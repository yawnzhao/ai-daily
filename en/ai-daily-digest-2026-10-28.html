World models

Genie 4 enters research preview: a world model starts generating picture, sound, and dialogue together

On October 27, Google DeepMind released the world model Genie 4. The company says it can generate interactive scenes in real time and natively generate ambient sound, music, and character dialogue. A user can move the view with a keyboard, or change the weather, the camera, or add people and objects with natural language while the scene runs. Gemini 3.8 Flash watches the picture in the loop, splits the goal, and checks whether each step is done.

The release is still limited. It is a research preview for trusted testers. Later it is planned for Google AI Ultra subscribers in Project Genie, and for early partners of the Gemini API and Google AI Studio. The company also lists limits: in a long session, after several complex edits, or while moving through a scene, detail and object persistence still drift. Complex physical interaction, real-time audio accuracy, clear text, and long-term consistency still need work. Generated video and audio receive a SynthID watermark. For robot and agent training it offers a more variable simulated environment. Simulated results still have to be checked in the real environment.

Scientific release

OpenAI publishes three scientific results, and the way they are released becomes its own dispute

OpenAI earlier published three model-produced results in mathematics, computer science, and physics, and said it would work with outside expert guides on how to release them. A follow-up in The Verge shows the three fields were treated differently. The model output for the mathematics result is public. The computer-science result will be explained by an outside research group. The physics result is not yet public. The computer-science result claims to overturn the exponential-time hypothesis, the idea that a class of logic problems cannot be solved significantly faster than brute-force search.

Researchers interviewed both think the results deserve to be taken seriously and criticize the company for letting a few chosen experts see the material first, which creates unequal access and delay. A mathematics and AI advisory group called for the natural-language proof and the Lean formalization to be released soon. MIT professor Ryan Williams, interviewed by TechCrunch, said two or three parts of the proof look unfamiliar and need the whole field to study them. He also asked whether existing cryptographic systems were affected, and relayed that OpenAI said a check left it fairly confident the result is not a break. These results are still under community review. They cannot be treated as confirmed by the field.

Drug development

Anthropic describes drug-development partnerships, while the listing filing also flags the timeline and the risk

Anthropic described drug-development work with six organizations over recent months, plus its own research projects. The cases cover cancer cell-therapy design, a food-allergy drug, antibody-fragment optimization, clinical-trial planning, and checks of manufacturing documents. Enveda connected production documents to Claude. The company says that in a test of 1,061 pages, Claude found all 11 problems human review had found and flagged 7 more that humans had missed. Anthropic also acquired the one-person company Mithril Medicine, which is developing a therapy for a rare muscular dystrophy.

The company itself stresses that most of this work is still preclinical and that the drugs have not entered human trials. WIRED, reading the listing filing, notes that the filing also says the company needs firsthand experiment and drug-discovery experience, has to handle hazardous materials, lab equipment, and regulatory compliance, and that the candidate drugs may never produce revenue. The change worth watching is that Anthropic treats biomedical discovery as a business direction, not only as a tool sold to drug companies. The steps a model can shorten still have to be separated from the steps that still need animal and human trials.

Public sector

An AI summary tool in UK asylum review may come under an independent inspection

WIRED reported that the UK's Independent Chief Inspector of Borders and Immigration has asked the digital-rights group Open Rights Group for material, to assess whether next year to inspect AI tools the Home Office uses in asylum review. The two tools summarize interview records of asylum applicants, and retrieve and summarize country-of-origin policy. Documents obtained by the group say they are based on OpenAI's 2023 GPT-4 model and Microsoft Foundry. The inspector's office said it cannot confirm specific topics before publishing the annual inspection priorities.

The dispute is about accuracy and transparency. The group says the share of summaries unfit for use is clearly higher than the roughly 9 percent published in the pilot evaluation. The tools were also not listed in the UK government's algorithm-transparency record, and applicants usually do not know their case used them. The Home Office replied that decisions are made by trained reviewers, that AI only helps browse records and policy material, and that it will publish an updated evaluation. For a public body, "a person still decides" does not by itself answer how a summary error is found, or how the person concerned can object.

01KAPPA checks statistical-paper proofs one claim at a time with Lean 4 and agents
Formal proofAnnounced October 27; v1 submitted October 26 at 15:22 UTCPreprint and stated limit that faithfulness to the paper still needs human review

Researchers at Purdue submitted a preprint on October 26, announced by arXiv on October 27, introducing the open-source tool KAPPA. It uses Claude Code to organize several language-model agents, translates proofs in a LaTeX paper claim by claim into Lean 4 and its Mathlib library for checking, and gives each numbered result one of four conclusions: verified unconditionally, verified conditionally under stated assumptions, a recorded problem and where it failed, or unfinished with a reason. It checks the paper's own proof step by step. It does not look for a different proof.

The authors applied the tool to four example papers that have answers, and to three published statistics papers that later issued corrections. For those three, they tested the pre-correction versions blind and report that the tool found an error at the step the correction pointed to, and found other problems later confirmed by people. They also state the limit: the Lean 4 kernel only checks the proof of the Lean 4 statement. Whether that statement is faithful to the paper still depends on review and human confirmation. Today's OpenAI mathematics result also puts formal proof in front. This paper offers a finer angle: what a machine check can prove, and what a person still has to confirm.

Using passages in ICLR 2026 peer review that explicitly discuss originality, the team built two groups of human ideas, with stronger and weaker novelty evidence, and tested six large-model reviewers.
