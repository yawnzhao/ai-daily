Speech systems

What a shopkeeper means when they pause

On November 20, Google Research described how Google Duplex came to make business calls through Tasks in Gemini. Users can describe what they need, including room for a stroller at a restaurant or a car-service appointment that fits around a meeting. Earlier systems needed separate training for different types of calls. The current model can consider the task, a receptionist’s proposed alternative and the user’s original constraints together.

The call has two ends. An espresso machine may drown out a shopkeeper, or a pause might just mean they are checking the appointment book. Google addresses acoustic interference and the rhythm of taking turns. It says the system identifies itself as an automated Google caller at the start. Businesses can opt out in their settings or ask it to stop during a call. The engineering account describes the architecture and its limits. It gives no independent success rate across tasks.

An actor licenses his face to play himself licensing his face

In interviews published by TechCrunch on November 21, the actor and production company described how Shortical licensed Aki Avni’s likeness to create a digital character for Inevitable. In the series, Avni plays himself licensing his face to a film studio. The premise captures a distinction that matters in the actual deal. Viewers can recognize an actor on screen even when the production and performance involve a different process from a live shoot.

The company signed this agreement directly with Avni. It also invites people to submit their social media accounts through its website, after which it can decide whether to discuss a contract. Shortical told the reporter that the public offering had yet to produce a signed deal at the time of the interview. The report gives neither Avni’s fee nor the full licensing terms. This project supplies no industry-wide price for a likeness.

How a self-hosted model can pick up where it left off

An AWS engineering article uses a resumed conversation to explain why a model might make a user wait. If a different server receives the new request, it may lack the key-value cache from the previous server and have to process the earlier text again. AWS describes a distributed cache that lets multiple servers share reusable intermediate states. The benefit depends on how often prefixes recur and where requests are routed. A one-off request with no shared prefix cannot assume the savings of a long-running conversation.

01What viewers see when a shared screen hides a name or replaces it
Screen privacyOriginal source, 2026-11-20Publisher-supplied abstract and bibliographic record via Crossref; full paper not reviewed.

The ScreenRecompose study, published November 20, compares masking, blurring and two forms of generative replacement. Participants watched videos and evaluated them from both screen sharers’ and viewers’ perspectives, judging how identifiable the information seemed and how natural the scene looked. Replacement with less dependence on the original semantics was harder to detect than masking. Perceived identifiability was lower than with blurring or the other replacement method. Replacement also helped participants understand the scene compared with masking.

These are measures of viewing experience. The video study isolated latency effects and cannot establish response time or privacy guarantees in live meetings. The authors also identify a risk of misinterpretation when substitutes look authentic. Yesterday’s reporting on screen privacy and today’s story about likeness licensing raise a related question. How can viewers tell which parts of an image have been altered?
