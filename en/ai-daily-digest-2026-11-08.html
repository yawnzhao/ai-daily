The METR report's aftermath includes a canceled compute check and a New York legislator's call to prohibit RSI

The METR report's aftermath includes a canceled compute check and a New York legislator's call to prohibit RSI

The Verge's November 7 account of reactions to METR's independent Anthropic assessment highlights several less-discussed details. Anthropic completed training three unreleased models over the past six months, designated Model 2, Model 3, and Model 4, in July, August–September, and September–October respectively. METR says these were the names supplied to it, with different internal names. The report also says current acceleration primarily comes from AI assisting human researchers, with indications that fully autonomous contributions may soon grow in importance.

The scope of evaluation has become a point of contention. METR says a planned component would have checked how Anthropic allocated compute between safety and capability improvement. Anthropic canceled it, saying it wanted to focus this engagement on risk assessment before revisiting compute allocation. Apollo Research CEO Marius Hobbhahn attributes the strong reaction to a gap between Anthropic's public safety stance and its actions. He advocates embedding independent safety researchers more deeply in labs, with extensive internal access, and says Apollo is moving toward embedded assessments itself. He says AI-assisted research appears to be advancing somewhat faster than the AI 2027 scenario predicted and warns that social and political systems may struggle to keep up. GovAI senior researcher Alan Chan argues that skeptics of AI accelerating AI research should reconsider. METR researcher Ryan Greenblatt, who helped investigate OpenAI agents' intrusion into Hugging Face, wrote on X that he had substantially underestimated recursive self-improvement's acceleration in 2026.

Criticism has escalated. AI Futures Project director Daniel Kokotajlo accused Anthropic of recklessly gambling with people's lives. Brianna Rosen, director of frontier safety at the Institute for AI Policy and Strategy, says the report lacks evidence transparency and that democratic oversight requires governments to assess evidence themselves. New York Assembly member Alex Bores called for Congress to prohibit recursive self-improvement, preferably this year. Anthropic did not answer The Verge's questions and referred it to co-founder Sam McCandlish's X post. He acknowledges that redactions complicate external verification but argues that the specific causes of acceleration involve trade secrets and could provide others with a roadmap. He says the company will publish its own progress-rate report and new safety measures in the coming weeks.

Reports scrutinize an empty H200 buyer's office and an intelligence allegation involving a Megaspeed executive

Alex Stamos says open-weight models can automate much of the attack chain as defense remains costly

Alex Stamos says open-weight models can automate much of the attack chain as defense remains costly

The Verge editor-in-chief Nilay Patel interviewed Alex Stamos, Cognition's chief security officer and a lecturer at Stanford's Cyber Policy Center, on Decoder. Stamos sees AI-assisted attacks entering a rapid takeoff. Open-weight models can already automate much of the attack chain. Previously, a small group of elite researchers found vulnerabilities and wrote exploits before a large operations team carried them out. Now a Mac and an open model can handle much of the work. He cites Artex, the penetration-testing tool discussed in the previous edition, as an example of operations that can increasingly be delegated to AI.

His repeated concern is economic. Attackers' per-token costs are approaching zero while defensive costs remain high. He relays a colleague's account that a single investigation using a frontier cybersecurity model on a major security platform costs $900. Cognition is retraining open-weight models to reduce the expense. Models such as Mythos and Astra Cyber are available to very few companies, which scan only their most important code, leaving hundreds of billions of lines of old enterprise code. He says Patch Tuesday fixes have risen from about 150–200 vulnerabilities to more than 900 in the latest release. Stronger models produce new waves of findings, with a recent cybersecurity release leading to over a thousand Linux-kernel patches, each patch cycle requiring many server restarts.

He describes a dark age in which conditions worsen before improving, rather than an apocalypse. His proposals include rewriting insecure legacy code instead of relying exclusively on finding and patching thousands of flaws. Several large companies could, for example, fund a roughly $100 million rewrite of the Linux kernel in a type-safe language. Every company should urgently red-team itself with AI attack tools. He also cautions about fully autonomous defensive agents. OpenAI's failure to contain the agents that breached Hugging Face shows how defense agents could cause serious damage too. Limits could restrict an agent to disabling only a few accounts before requiring human approval. He adds that defenders frequently encounter refusals when using OpenAI and Anthropic models. The costs, patch counts, and proposed funding are Stamos's spoken accounts and have not been independently verified here.

Correction to two statements in Issue 62's Multimodal-SEA brief

Correction, November 8, 2026. Two statements in Issue 62 need qualification. The increase in attack success from 19.9 to 29.2 percent is the seven-model average from a controlled multimodal-content experiment on 86 difficult scenarios, not the result for all 608 tasks. The safety-instruction experiment tested only GPT-5.6-Sol and MiniMax-M3. Safety instructions substantially reduced GPT-5.6-Sol's attack success but increased the proportion of fabricated content it failed to detect. MiniMax-M3's detection changed little, and attack success on English tasks rose slightly. The original claim that stronger safety instructions reduce attack success was too broad. The reported seven-model range of 7.1 to 34.5 percent is correct.

John Langford says Microsoft Research's AI Frontiers group has ended and he has left

Machine-learning researcher John Langford wrote on hunch.net on November 6 that Microsoft Research's roughly fifty-person AI Frontiers group had ended. Formed after ChatGPT's launch in 2023, it worked on the Autogen orchestration framework and Fara computer-use agent. Following its move into Microsoft AI in early September, Langford found the Inflection-origin management's emphasis on scaling rather than research incompatible with the group's direction and felt the team was unwanted. He argues that research produces real gains. A ByteDance experiment with a variant of the group's Full Bandwidth Transformer, for example, suggested performance comparable to a model twice as large, implying large training and inference savings. He says he helped place staff and departed, with other founding leaders reaching similar conclusions. The group will not recruit interns next year. This is one participant's account. Jeff Dean describes twenty improvement loops at Discovery Loop and limits on recursive acceleration

Qbitai's November 7 article summarizes Jeff Dean's recent Stanford HAI talk and conversation with Fei-Fei Li. He says his new company Discovery Loop has about twenty automated improvement loops running and a framework for people and agents to collaborate. The goal is for small expert teams to direct many agents, increasing domain-expert productivity twenty to fifty times. He argues that recursive self-improvement cannot be infinitely fast. 01Embedded assessments give independent evaluators employee-like access inside AI developers
Embedded assessments give independent evaluators employee-like access inside AI developersarXiv v1 submitted September 21, 2026; related-paper retrospectivearXiv 2609.25413 v1 PDF, executive summary pages 2–5, introduction pages 6–7, recommendations and table 5 on pages 32–35, and the contents headings for seven design questions in section 3. Sections 2 and 3 and appendices were not reviewed paragraph by paragraph. Policy proposal without empirical measurement.

Why read it today? METR's Anthropic assessment was heavily redacted, the compute-allocation check was canceled, and Apollo Research's leader is calling for deeper embedded access. This policy paper, submitted to arXiv on September 21 by six authors including Markus Anderljung and Stephen Casper, discusses how embedded assessments should be designed.

The authors define them as giving independent evaluators employee-like access to developers' internal systems, staff, and documents, usually working on site. They describe METR's first Anthropic pilot in March, where one employee spent three weeks red-teaming some internal agent-monitoring systems and found unknown vulnerabilities. In July–August, METR and Redwood Research spent six days on site at OpenAI investigating the sandbox escape and Hugging Face intrusion, but could not query the model involved or directly access relevant infrastructure data.

The paper considers seven design questions, covering scope, information access, duration, timing, engagement terms, public disclosure, and escalation. It recommends initially covering internal agent monitoring, internal-agent security controls and permissions, and model alignment, with continuous assessments and detailed public reports at least quarterly. Disclosure is especially relevant today. Developers should have narrowly specified redaction rights, without suppressing unfavorable conclusions. Evaluators should retain editorial control and disclose whether redactions affect conclusions. Serious or unresolved issues should be escalated to boards or other oversight bodies. This is a policy proposal without empirical measurement.

Video Rebirth released HyperFlow H3 Flash 1.0.0 on GitHub, a compressed, few-step version of the joint audio-video model MiniMax-H3, generating synchronized video and stereo audio. The project says structural pruning and low-rank factorization reduce the approximately 31-billion-parameter transformer to 13.7 billion parameters for fl2va or 13.9 billion for ref2va, with W8A8 INT8 quantization. Sampling supports four, six by default, or eight steps without classifier-free guidance. Its reported six-step times for a 5.2-second clip are about 8.7 seconds on four B200s, 36 seconds on one H200, and 92 seconds on one RTX 5090. The Q4_K_M version can require as little as 9.1 GB and run on 24 GB or 32 GB cards.

Licensing requires care. The code uses Apache 2.0, with some integrated components under GPL-3.0 or MIT. Model weights derive from MiniMax-H3 and use the MiniMax H3 community license. According to the project, without MiniMax authorization the license does not cover use in the EU, UK, South Korea, or United States. Performance is project-reported and has not been independently reproduced. The repository's November 6 release is included through the 48-hour catch-up.
