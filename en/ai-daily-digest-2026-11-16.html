Human–computer interaction

Who decides when a thirsty plant should frown?

Before you water a plant, you could check its leaves or glance at a little face frowning beside the pot. On November 15, WIRED interviewed Daehwa Kim, a Carnegie Mellon University researcher who developed OATs with Chris Harrison. The system pairs small E Ink displays with sensors on everyday objects. You name the object, choose what it can sense and edit the rules the system generates for its behavior. In the same dry room, a plant that thrives in dry air and one that prefers humidity can wear different expressions.

In the study, 12 people took the characters they had configured home for a week. They tried them on refrigerators, lamps and exercise equipment. One participant rearranged food in the fridge after seeing the feedback. Others found the faces cute but saw little reason to exercise on their account. The researchers also documented how hard it could be to position sensors or interpret an expression. Turning a thermometer into a face can make someone stop and look. It will take a longer study to see whether they still look after the first week.

Chips and export policy

U.S. lawmakers ask where chips go and who can use them remotely

On November 15, The Verge examined the questions Congress raised about chip exports after NVIDIA's leadership change. Senate Banking Committee chair Tim Scott wrote to Jensen Huang and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick asking them to testify. A House Foreign Affairs Committee letter sought answers about chip smuggling, foreign military use of NVIDIA products and reports of interference in export investigations. The letter sets out questions lawmakers want investigated.

The Remote Access Security Act, which has drawn renewed attention, addresses a different feature of computing. A chip can stay in a data center while someone operates it from another country. The House version of H.R. 2683 would extend export-control authority to remote access to controlled items in specified circumstances. The House passed that text in January. The weekend requests for testimony, the bill that passed one chamber and export rules already in force each carry a different legal status.

China's consultation on technology export restrictions closes

China's Ministry of Commerce and Ministry of Science and Technology set October 16 through November 15 as the public comment period for Notice No. 48. The proposed revisions cover AI, biomedicine and photovoltaics. Under the notice, exporters must register before exporting the listed technologies from the date of the notice until the final revisions take effect. November 15 marked the deadline for comments.

In an accompanying Q&A, the Ministry of Commerce used large models to explain the proposed scope. Deploying a model in the restricted category locally overseas and giving overseas users access through an API can involve different transfers of technology. The question in each case is whether a listed technology moves from China to another country. The ministry also distinguished technology already in the public domain from technology that might later be exported through an open-source release.

An AI host introduced a remix. The player served up the original

WIRED reporter Reece Rogers tried YouTube Music's experimental Beyond the Beat and Spotify's DJ. On YouTube Music, the host introduced a remix and its guest artist, then played the original recording, on which that artist never appeared. On another occasion the synthetic host mispronounced Charli XCX. The mismatch was specific enough to pinpoint what went wrong in the programming: the introduction belonged to a different recording.

Spotify announced new voices and controls for adjusting the commentary on November 10. Rogers found its introductions tighter, though he still preferred to switch them off. Both services let listeners choose whether to use the feature. One listener's experience offers a concrete test for either product. Does the introduction match the actual recording queued to play?

South Korean tax accountant exam candidate admits using generative AI to cheat

A candidate who ranked first in South Korea's tax accountant qualification exam apologized publicly through a lawyer on November 15, Yonhap News Agency reported. Can a coding agent earn wider permissions the way a new colleague does?

On Y Combinator's Lightcone, Lauren Tan and Roman Ugarte described building and deploying Grok Bot. They discussed automated tests, guardrails in the codebase, and the identity and permissions assigned to the bot. Their approach gives the agent a limited remit at first. Results that colleagues can inspect provide a basis for widening it.

Chapters on identity and permissions, getting an agent to check its own work, and codebase guardrails put the idea into practice. A patch that passes a test gives reviewers evidence about that change. Whether to give the agent permission to edit the test itself is a separate decision. The original episode appeared November 14. 01When math gets hard, how do students respond to a human tutor and an AI tutor?
Learning sciencesPublished November 13, revisited November 16.Paper Radar retrospective based on the authors’ abstract and DOI record. The experiment was not replicated.

Megan Ennion and Ros McLellan randomly assigned students aged 16 to 18 at one school in England to a human tutor working one to one, a generative AI tutor or independent web search. Students completed two sets of challenging math problems. After the second set, those with an AI tutor reported putting in more effort and finding the problems harder than those with a human tutor. The human-tutored group attempted the most problems in both sets. Which pairwise differences reached statistical significance varied by set.

The groups showed no significant difference in willingness to take on a subsequent challenge. That willingness was closely related to students' existing tendency to seek challenges. Attempts, correct answers and willingness to try another task each tell a teacher something different. Published November 13 and included in today's Paper Radar, the study sits alongside the South Korean exam story for a reason: a finished answer sheet reveals little about how a student completed the task. This account draws on the authors' abstract and describes responses within the study task.

British Journal of Educational Technology | Published November 13, revisited November 16.

Datasette's model plugin can connect directly to compatible endpointsApache-2.0

Simon Willison's datasette-llm 0.1a11 lets developers configure endpoints that speak the OpenAI protocol directly. In the official example, Datasette calls a model through a local service. Developers specify a local identifier, the actual model name and the API address. This lets them connect their database tools to a service of their choice without installing a separate model plugin for each compatible endpoint.

The release notes also spell out how credentials travel. The plugin looks up a credential when the endpoint specifies its name. An endpoint configured without one receives no default provider key. Protocol compatibility makes the connection possible. How reliably tools run and how accurately they query data still depend on the implementation. The plugin uses the Apache-2.0 license.

2
LangChain lets developers choose a model gateway for a single requestMIT

LangChain's langchain-core 1.8.1 adds the langsmith_gateway context manager for selecting a gateway. As the maintainer explains in the change notes, when a chat model is created inside that context, the routing choice applies only to the current thread or asynchronous task. Setting the gateway through an environment variable previously affected the whole process.

Timing matters here. Routing is set when the model is created, so the model creation step belongs inside the context. Creating the model first and entering the context only when sending a request is too late. The change applies to integrations that use the shared resolver.
