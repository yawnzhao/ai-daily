# AI Daily No. 78 · You asked an assistant to check your statement. Whose instructions did the bank receive?

Wells Fargo’s new agreement spells out how it will treat instructions from AI assistants. OpenAI’s chief scientist proposes tracking pretraining progress with perplexity, and OpenRouter changes which endpoints qualify for zero data retention.

### Whose instructions count when an AI assistant uses your Wells Fargo account?

Section 15 of Wells Fargo’s online access agreement, effective November 24, deals specifically with AI assistants. WIRED reported on the revision on November 22. Under the bank’s terms, if customers give an assistant their login credentials or otherwise authorize it to act, the bank may treat its instructions as authorized by the customer. Unless the law requires otherwise, the bank need not check each instruction to see whether the assistant understood what the customer meant.

Customers are responsible for monitoring the assistant’s activity and promptly revoking its access. The bank reserves the right to restrict particular transactions, change how assistants connect or cut off their access. The terms stop short of a blanket ban. Asking to see a statement, granting access to an account and sending the bank an instruction are three separate steps. A single chat message may say very little about the last two.

[WIRED](https://www.wired.com/story/banks-are-cracking-down-on-ai-agents/)

### OpenAI’s chief scientist proposes perplexity as a way to compare pretraining progress

In a November 22 article, OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki shared results from tracking pretrained models on a fixed sample of internal code. Perplexity measures how well a model predicts the next token on average. A lower score indicates better prediction. Pachocki proposes using the metric alongside other measures when developers discuss shared safety standards, including what monitoring capabilities they should have in place before scaling up further.

The proposal leaves an important measurement question open. Teams use different test material and training data, so the same score can reflect tasks of different difficulty. Pachocki also points out that reasoning techniques can improve task performance without lowering pretraining perplexity. The historical scores in the article depend on the sample and how they were calculated. On their own, they tell us little about a model’s reliability on a particular real-world task.

[OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/perplexity-and-intelligence)

### OpenRouter revises eligibility for zero data retention

In a November 22 notice, OpenRouter said researchers had identified data-handling problems at some inference providers. The platform stopped routing requests to three providers and removed 125 endpoints from its zero-data-retention list. That total includes endpoints the researchers flagged and others the platform removed as a precaution. It counts endpoints, not separate leaks. OpenRouter said it had notified potentially affected customers and would require appropriate assurances and verification before restoring eligibility. It said the findings to date showed no evidence of unauthorized access to OpenRouter itself.

[OpenRouter](https://openrouter.ai/blog/announcements/data-processing-incidents-on-inference-providers/)

### Why data centers have become a focus of local disputes

In a November 22 TechCrunch interview, Paris Marx, author of *Hyperscale*, recalled visiting a data center in New Zealand. He had to submit his passport details in advance and pass through access-controlled doors on his way to the server room. Inside, he saw rows of racks. Outside, the questions that mattered to neighbors concerned electricity, water and the noise that rooftop cooling equipment might make.

Marx argues that effects people can experience where they live give them something more tangible to debate than promises about technology. The interview offers his observations and interpretation. It provides no common figures from which to estimate electricity bills, water use or noise across different communities.

[TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/22/why-data-centers-are-at-the-center-of-the-big-tech-backlash/)

### Would students join an environmental campaign recommended by AI?

A journal study published November 19 asked undergraduates at one Vietnamese university to imagine a platform recommending an environmental, social and governance (ESG) campaign. Of the 218 people who participated, 199 provided responses that met the researchers’ criteria. Using structural equation modeling, the team found that students who rated the system as more capable also tended to trust it more. Among the standardized associations the researchers compared, the strongest was between attitude and intention to participate.

Today’s data center interview raises the question of how people respond to environmental issues. The study helps pin down what willingness to take part can mean in a survey. Students considered a hypothetical recommendation. They did not use a working recommender system, and the researchers did not track sign-ups or attendance. The findings concern associations among students’ own reported views.

[Computers in Human Behavior Reports](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chbr.2026.101474)

### Armin Ronacher on coding assistants and the projects worth keeping

In a talk released by AI Engineer on November 22, Armin Ronacher draws on records of his own work with the Pi coding assistant. According to the publisher’s description, his tools changed little, but as the models improved, the assistant could work on tasks for longer and needed fewer corrections from him. The chapter list returns to questions of human attention, when to challenge an assistant’s work and which projects deserve continued maintenance. The examples in the description and chapters come from Ronacher’s projects and give no general measure of developer productivity.

### A model bookstore shows Muru’s author when lazy loading can slow things down

In a November 22 engineering note, Muru author Graham Dumpleton uses a small Flask storefront to compare two ways of loading modules. A command-line tool asked only to display help can skip modules it does not need. Some servers, by contrast, load an application before forking workers so the processes can share what is already in memory. Deferring a frequently used module until the first request can leave each worker to pay the loading cost separately the first time it needs that module.

The sample store uses different libraries for inventory reports and spreadsheet downloads. Dumpleton uses Muru’s measurements to identify which modules the store needs at startup, which serve occasional requests and which processes bear the cost. The article shows how to make that judgment for a particular deployment and traffic pattern. We did not run the example.

[Graham Dumpleton](https://grahamdumpleton.me/posts/2026/11/muru-when-lazy-imports-help/)
