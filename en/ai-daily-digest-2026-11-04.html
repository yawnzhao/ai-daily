Anthropic's updated filing says it may be "approaching" the threshold for AI that markedly speeds research, and compute is now 58 percent of revenue

On November 2, Anthropic filed an updated S-1, extending the financial disclosure through September 30. TechCrunch notes two new layers in the risk factors. First, the company is using AI more and more to speed its own research, which could let model capability pull ahead of the development and testing of safety, alignment, and safeguards. The company says it has in the past failed to recognize, and may in the future fail to recognize, a marked acceleration in research speed until it has already happened. Second, on internal estimates and expectations for future models rather than models that exist now, the company may be approaching the definition in its Responsible Scaling Policy of significant acceleration of progress brought about by AI. The filing also says no current model has been found to cross that threshold, the company is still assessing the data, and it does not expect to be able to let Claude fully and autonomously design and build the next frontier model without meaningful human supervision or understanding. It is considering an update to the policy so humans stay in control of frontier-model development. TechCrunch also notes that an S-1 is both a disclosure and a marketing document. The quoted sense follows the Chinese edition's account of the filing. The S-1 itself was not opened.

The same filing's numbers are just as striking. The Verge reports that in the nine months through September 30, Anthropic's revenue was 33.24 billion dollars, up 1200 percent year over year, with a net loss of 104 billion dollars, most of it from accounting charges. Third-quarter revenue alone was about 17 billion dollars, with a net loss of 23.29 billion dollars, plus 895.4 billion dollars of spending commitments, mainly for compute and infrastructure. TechCrunch's analysis says third-quarter compute spending was about 10 billion dollars, 58 percent of revenue, up from 48 percent the quarter before. Revenue per dollar of compute fell back to 1.72 dollars from a peak a little above 2 dollars the previous quarter. The filing explains that 5 billion dollars of that came from short-term compute commitments, priced well above multi-year contracts. TechCrunch notes that Anthropic has been paying SpaceX 1.25 billion dollars a month for compute, and has multi-billion-dollar contracts with newer cloud providers such as Nscale and Akamai. That compute is more flexible and also more expensive. The company is pushing a self-built compute plan, Anthropic Direct, but it expects compute and infrastructure to remain the largest operating expense for the foreseeable future.

Research

An MIT team says GPT-6 Astra can read and write neural-network weights directly; critics say the headline result used code

Rikiya Takehi, Jayden Teoh, Alex Zhang, and Omar Khattab of MIT CSAIL published a long post on November 2, "Sparks of Weight-Space Programming". They say the frontier model GPT-6 Astra can read and modify the raw weights of a neural network. They report giving Astra, as text, four small game-policy networks trained with reinforcement learning, identical in structure, about 17,000 weights each. With no options offered and no code allowed to run, it named the game each network played. It could also write, from scratch, a network that plays a small game. The result that drew the most attention is experiment 8: making Qwen3.5-0.8B better at math without gradients. Astra found one direction that made the model fall into a repetition loop, changed 0.016 percent of the weights, and, under the same sampling settings, lifted MATH-500 from 7.9 percent to 48.1 percent, with IFEval rising about threefold. The authors also say that on Gemma 3 270M, which does not fall into loops as readily, Astra's edits did not improve results by more than about one percentage point. Removing an obstacle that was hiding ability is not the same as adding ability the model did not have.

The post spread quickly in the discussion of recursive self-improvement, and it was questioned just as quickly. A post on honnibal.dev argues that the abstract is misleading. The setting that really looks only at raw weights, with no tools, appears only in experiments 1, 2, and 4, on networks of about 17,000 to 172,000 weights. Every experiment that involves a real language model used Python, and the Qwen headline result could also run the model on up to 50 batches of practice problems. Another post, on seangoedecke.com, unpacks what Astra did in the Qwen experiment: write a few sets of text that loop and text that does not, compare the internal differences, find the weights that contribute most, then cut them down step by step and test. The author thinks that is exactly what a capable human machine-learning researcher would do. Impressive, and not superhuman. Developer Simon Willison tried the "look at the weights, guess the game" prompt with Qwen 3.8 27B running on a laptop, got three of the four games right, and missed the relatively obscure Freeway. He also thinks a person with experience, given enough time with paper and pencil, could do it. The work is so far a research report in blog form. The authors have published the prompts, the logs, and the edited model. This issue did not reproduce it.

Google: AI tools on student accounts will be off by default, but a school that refuses AI entirely will also lose Google search

On November 3, Google announced a change to AI in its education products. From next year, Gemini, Gemini in Classroom, and other AI tools on K-12 student accounts, along with Google search, will be off by default unless a school administrator turns them on. TechCrunch reports that Google says the change came from talks with schools, teachers, researchers, and policymakers. The catch is that a school cannot turn off AI and keep search. Chris Phillips, Google's vice president for education, said search and AI are deeply integrated. Students at a school that restricts AI across the board will not be able to use search, and they will see an "unavailable" notice from the school. The reported remarks follow the Chinese edition's account.

The Verge reports that the arrangement drew criticism. U.S. senator Bernie Sanders described it as extortion. New York mayor Zohran Mamdani said no company should have the power to decide how we educate children. Google says the change gives schools more choice, not less, and that each service can be switched separately. Google also said it will put Guided Learning, meant to walk students through a problem step by step, into AI Overviews in search, with a test at some U.S. schools this month. Only a few months ago, Google turned a Gemini assistant on by default in Classroom, an app used by more than 150 million students and teachers worldwide. For a school, the choice is between accepting AI inside search and giving up search. Chromebooks and Google apps are common enough in class that the choice is not an easy one. Google's own blog was not opened separately.

01A paper: SelfImprove-Bench, and how far a self-improving agent's gains actually travel
Evaluationv1 submitted November 2, 2026; 48-hour catch-upThe arXiv abstract page for v1; the full paper was not read

A preprint submitted to arXiv on November 2, "SelfImprove-Bench: Evaluating Generalization in Self-Improving Agents" (arXiv:2611.02797, v1), asks a question that sits next to today's news. When a recursive self-improvement system gets better, is that real ability, or overfitting to the training setting? The authors argue that current evaluations lack a reliable measure for separating a gain in ability from a fit to the scene, and lack a protocol that can keep scaling to unseen tasks and difficulties.

They organize tasks by three abilities: exploration, planning, and reasoning. Games implement the tasks so the setup stays controllable and reproducible. Templates and a difficulty factor generate tasks in bulk. Evaluation is a generalization ladder from G0 to G4, measuring how far a gain travels from the training conditions. The authors report that the evolutionary gains of current self-improvement methods are not consistent across those levels. The reading scope is the abstract. Methods and figures should be taken from the full paper. This is a preprint and has not been peer reviewed.

Hugging Face adds a Model Integration Contract to Transformers: a shrunken model catches quiet bugs on every pull requestNone

On November 3, Hugging Face described a new testing practice for the Transformers library, a Model Integration Contract. The prompt was a quiet bug. On July 30, after Transformers turned on PyTorch scaled dot-product attention for T5, UMT5's decoder could peek at future tokens during training. The code did not crash, and the bug sat on the main branch for nearly two months.

A contract has three parts. A small model, shrunk to the real checkpoint's configuration but keeping every layer type, published once and pinned to a version. A runnable example of typical use. And an output baseline a person has reviewed. The contract holds if the same use on the small model still produces the same result. The team replayed the frozen contracts over five months of commits. A small UMT5 running on CPU raised an alarm on every commit that contained the bug. The team also notes that a version running only eager attention would have missed this bug. The test model has to walk the same code path as the real model. Shrinking a model is not just deleting layers. An architecture that mixes sliding-window and full attention, or dense and mixture-of-experts layers, has to keep every layer type. These small models do not replace a full integration test.
