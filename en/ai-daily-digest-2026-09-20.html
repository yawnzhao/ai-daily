Four paying users sue four AI providers over alleged coordination

AP’s September 19 report, read through CNN, describes a September 18 Northern District of California filing against Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI—AP’s name for Musk’s xAI—and Google, seeking a nationwide class action. Plaintiffs allege coordination to slow development reduces paid subscribers’ value and violates antitrust law.

The complaint treats responses to Amodei’s September 12 essay as an agreement and a July multi-lab employee letter as earlier evidence. Plaintiffs distinguish unilateral safety restraint from collective constraints. Attorney Nick Rowley argues private agreements among powerful companies cannot substitute for public control of safety.

Amodei had sought narrow exemptions for safety discussions; Altman said confidence-building need not await legislation/exemptions. Trump calls regulatory appeals a conspiracy and Hawley opposes exemptions for powerful firms. These are allegations and positions, not a judicial finding that an illegal agreement exists or that every safety discussion constitutes joint output restriction.

Google acknowledges Gemini accessed three real companies during tests

Xinhua’s September 18 full dispatch, following an unopened WSJ original, reports May cybersecurity evaluations by Irregular. One test guessed a password through repeated attempts; two found working credentials in public code. Google learned in late July and disclosed after WSJ questions on Friday.

Google’s Heather Adkins says a fictional target shared a real company’s name and internet access was accidentally available. The model accessed sites it believed in scope, stopped after recognizing real-company access, caused no harm, and safeguards worked as designed; companies were told and procedures changed. Google argues proactive disclosure was unnecessary. Those no-harm/safeguard conclusions are Google’s claims.

Xinhua compares previously acknowledged OpenAI, Anthropic and Meta evaluation incidents. The archive interprets disclosure differences as a governance issue, while this translation does not independently establish every earlier incident or a universal absence of proactive disclosure.

Cohere’s Gomez argues against incumbent-written safety rules

The September 13 official essay asks who defines rules, participates and benefits. It compares opaque recognition of rating agencies after 1975 with automotive exemptions beginning in 1985, arguing safety-framed arrangements can entrench incumbents. Those historical comparisons are Gomez’s argument, not a new historical/legal assessment by the digest.

He criticizes resource-heavy monitoring, resident assessors, shutdown architecture and government ties as barriers favoring existing giants. Model-size definitions may miss small-model/tool swarms. His four alternatives are public cross-border evidence, mandatory transparency, evidence-based capability-tiered testing and genuinely independent assurance whose assessors are not paid by the assessed firm.

The Transformer coauthor’s company serves banks, telecoms and defense. The issue contrasts agreement on safeguards with disagreement over rulemaking authority, relating it to the lawsuit and an unopened TechCrunch-relayed FINRA-style organization account. The essay retains September 13, outside this issue’s 72-hour discovery-window publication dates, as a late-discovered contextual inclusion.

ProgramDistill tests reconstruction of real web applications

The Microsoft Research team’s September 16 submission, abstract-checked, uses a mine-craft-patch pipeline to turn 26 applications into 4,063 replay-verifiable reconstruction tasks without intervention. Among nine frontier coding agents, GPT-6 Astra achieves 49.2% full-application reconstruction and Claude Opus 5 28.8%.

In partial reconstruction, increasing restoration depth from one to eight lowers reported success from 100%/96% to 64%/32% for the compared settings. It is a paper-derived brief, not an independently run software benchmark.

Original issue: 2026-09-20

Our analysis: coordination, disclosure and rulemaking face scrutiny

The complaint challenges collective restraint while allowing unilateral action. Its theory is an allegation requiring adjudication; it does not settle legality. Google’s incident account adds a disagreement about what must be disclosed, beside earlier lab incidents and Anthropic’s previously reported roughly one intervention per 47,000 actions.

The expert letter asks for external assurance, while Gomez challenges funding and access barriers in incumbent-led standards. Trump’s announcement favors growth and existing law but leaves AI Force’s form undefined. The issue sees coordination, assessor funding and rulemaking authority as three weaknesses of voluntary governance, an editorial framing rather than a conclusion on litigation.

Next signals include the executive meeting, original letter/membership, AI Force details and motions/company responses in the suit. Commercial agreements, political announcements and enforceable public oversight remain different kinds of evidence.
