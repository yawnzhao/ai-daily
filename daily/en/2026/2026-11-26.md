### The FCC clears SpaceX to deploy up to one million orbital computing satellites

On November 25, the US Federal Communications Commission approved SpaceX’s application for its Starmind constellation. The license allows up to one million orbital computing modules to process data in space for use on Earth. That figure sets the upper limit on deployment. To measure the computing power available to customers, the satellites first have to launch, connect to one another and enter service.

A data center in one orbit needs a way to reach its neighbors. The FCC announcement describes optical links between satellites, with radio links for backup communications and emergency telemetry, tracking and control. The authorization settles what SpaceX may deploy and how the network may communicate. How much computing capacity it can deliver consistently to users on the ground will depend on the constellation in operation.

### After the IPO, Anthropic’s CEO argues that people should keep the power to govern

Yesterday’s issue covered Anthropic’s shares closing below their offering price. On November 25, CEO Dario Amodei responded to reporting on a different question about the company. Should people hand governing authority to AI? Amodei said he opposed the idea and rejected the argument that efficiency or safety could justify taking away people’s right to decide for themselves.

He drew a parallel with democracy. Someone might reach a decision faster, but speed alone gives them no authority to make it for everyone else. Amodei applied that reasoning to AI and argued that legitimate democratic processes should determine how it is governed. His statement sets out his own position following the listing.

### 42 attorneys general challenge a proposed sale of private Character.AI chats

When Character.AI entered bankruptcy, the proposed asset sale included both the platform and the private conversations people had held on it. California Attorney General Rob Bonta’s office said a coalition of 42 attorneys general opposed the terms. The data include chat histories from more than 21 million US accounts. More than 2.1 million of those accounts were registered to minors.

In their court filing, the attorneys general asked that consumer protections be addressed before a transfer. They pointed to what the conversations contain, what the associated account information could reveal and what users had been promised about privacy. A court can consider the right to acquire the software separately from the right to read old conversations. At the time of review, the coalition had filed its objection and the proposed sale was still before the court.

### ChatGPT tests a shared allowance for Chat and Work. Its heaviest users may hit the limit sooner

OpenAI product lead Thibault “Tibo” Sottiaux confirmed that a small group of Pro users had begun testing a combined Chat and Work interface. An OpenAI spokesperson told TechCrunch that activity in the two areas would draw on one allowance. OpenAI expects most users to hit limits about as often as before. A small group with the highest usage may reach the cap earlier.

Conversations using Instant do not count toward the shared allowance. Users can keep chatting with Instant after they exhaust it. For someone who previously switched between Chat and Work, the change affects both where they go to do a task and how their more demanding work is counted.

### New York signs a five-year ban on chatbot toys. Holiday shopping comes first

On November 24, Governor Kathy Hochul signed a law imposing a five-year ban on making, distributing or selling covered internet-connected chatbot toys in New York. The law takes effect 90 days after signing. With the holiday shopping season already starting, Hochul urged parents and caregivers to consider the privacy and safety risks when choosing toys. TechCrunch followed up on November 25 on how the restrictions would apply.

A toy can play a recorded nursery rhyme when a child presses a button. Another can send the child’s words to an online service that generates a new reply. Both boxes might say the toy talks. Asking how it comes up with its next line tells a parent more about what they are buying.

### Scientists ask how anyone would spot the evidence an AI research assistant left out

Researchers at the Allen Institute for AI (Ai2) interviewed 17 scientists in the natural, applied and social sciences about the work they might delegate to an AI agent. They discussed time-consuming data processing, coding and research records. Across those tasks, the scientists wanted enough of a record to check what the tool had actually done.

Consider a literature review. Every sentence in a summary could carry a real citation even if the search skipped papers that challenged the hypothesis. The interview team proposed designing evaluations with scientists to examine how an agent searches for evidence, records its steps and lets people intervene. Those evaluations still need to be developed and tested.

### Health AI warnings lowered trust. Did they help people judge when to rely on an answer?

Jiawei Zhou and colleagues randomly assigned 500 participants to five groups in a preregistered experiment. One group saw a familiar general disclaimer. The other four saw structured risk disclosures. The researchers varied whether the wording was specific to health and whether it explained how the model worked. They measured trust in the model separately from confidence in one’s own judgment.

Participants who received the structured disclosures reported lower trust in the model. Their confidence in judging when to rely on it showed no statistically significant difference from the comparison group. The study measured what participants reported. It did not track medical decisions or health outcomes. Whether a warning gives someone enough information to choose their next step remains an open question.

Jane Mann, Chief Education Officer at Cambridge University Press & Assessment, hosted this November 23 conversation with Andreas Schleicher, the OECD’s Director for Education and Skills. Heard alongside this issue’s experiment on risk warnings, the conversation suggests a question for teachers looking at a completed assignment. What further evidence would show that the student could make the next choice independently?

### llama.cpp lets several input copies share a single wait

Release b12732 of llama.cpp changes how it copies inputs. Previously, when part of a computation needed several inputs from the host, the software made a blocking copy of each in turn. The new path can queue several asynchronous copies and then synchronize once. Each input still has to be ready by the time the computation uses it.

Backends have to opt in. CUDA is the only one to do so in this release. Other backends keep the earlier behavior, and pipeline parallelism has separate synchronization requirements. The project uses the MIT license. For developers tracing latency in local inference, pull request #30685 shows exactly where to look for time spent waiting on input copies. Any savings will depend on the workload and hardware.
