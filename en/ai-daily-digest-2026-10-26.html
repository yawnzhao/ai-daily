Enterprise adoption and productivity

Models got cheaper. Company ledgers still do not show a clear improvement

TechCrunch reported on October 25 that Apollo chief economist Torsten Slok questioned the financial payoff of AI spending outside the giant technology companies. In an original comment on October 24, he compared revenue per employee, margins, and related measures for large technology companies with the rest of the S&P 500, and argued that the latter had not shown the same improvement.

This is an economist's reading of aggregate data. It cannot be pushed into a claim that every company and every AI application has no benefit. It raises a question worth following: writing faster and calling a model more cheaply, when does that become higher revenue, a lower total cost, or more stable delivery? Business integration, human judgment, organizational coordination, and rework still sit in between. Measuring adoption cannot stop at counting model calls.

Open-source community governance

r/opensource tightens submissions of AI projects, and a community draws a line around maintenance

The Verge reported on October 25 that Reddit's r/opensource community published new submission rules. Projects that use AI, are made by AI, or are aimed at AI are not welcome, and the rules raise requirements for how long a project has existed, ongoing maintenance, and contributors. The report shows moderators' concern about low-quality submissions, and it also records user opposition and the response of starting another community.

The rules also involve language and moderation capacity. Moderators said it is hard to tell AI-generated text from AI translation, and the restriction would affect people who do not write in English. Assistive uses have exceptions and discretion, so the headline cannot be simplified into a ban on every AI feature.

This is one community's decision about submissions and governance. It is not a change in the definition of open source or in a software license. What is worth watching is who does the reading, the answering, the maintenance, and the filtering once projects become easier to produce.

01A related paper: show people which thinking they handed to AI
Human-AI collaboration and learningSeptember 17, 2026Authors' reported experiment on a short arithmetic task; historical related look-back, not a new paper from this window

Why read it today: discussions of company payoff and AI cost both ask how a tool's capability becomes real capability. This September 17 preprint in the Paper-Radar library, Designing Against Deskilling: Metacognitive Feedback Reduces Cognitive Offloading to LLM Assistants, puts the same question in a learning interface. Getting an answer and learning it yourself are not the same result. This is a historical related look-back. It is not a new paper from today, and it is not counted in the 36-hour source-catalog total.

The authors had 704 participants practice fraction arithmetic and then take a test without AI help. While the ability to ask for help remained, they compared metacognitive feedback, an effort reward, and the corresponding controls. The feedback reminded participants how they had just used AI and which practice had been replaced by a complete answer. The authors report that this feedback reduced requests for a complete answer and improved the later independent test. The effort reward did not show the same effect.

The important limit is that the study did not find that merely providing AI access significantly lowered independent-test performance. The experiment is short and uses a specific arithmetic task. It cannot be pushed into a claim that all AI makes people worse, or that one prompt improves long-term job performance. What it offers is a design direction that can be tested: let the user decide how much thinking to delegate, rather than only chase a faster answer.
