### ChatGPT Wallet lets an agent shop and check out, with approval for every payment

OpenAI introduced ChatGPT Wallet on November 13. Its dots agent can select items and pay for them. The initial rollout is for US ChatGPT Pro subscribers aged 18 or over. According to OpenAI's explanation to The Verge, customers also need a US billing address and an eligible US-issued American Express, Chase, Mastercard or Visa card.

A customer adds a card once. Before each checkout, they can see the items, total and card, then authorize payment using Face ID or a fingerprint, for example. A payment token protects the card number from both the agent and the merchant. The merchant receives the shipping address and contact information. OpenAI says cardholders keep rewards and protections such as the ability to dispute a transaction.

OpenAI plans to let customers preauthorize purchases within spending, category or time limits. Merchant acceptance is a current constraint. Some retailers restrict or block shopping agents, most notably Amazon, according to TechCrunch. OpenAI also released a Commerce Partner Playbook for merchants. TechCrunch reported that DoorDash and Uber have seen very few agent orders so far, while Meta's Muse has passed 10 million users and AI-assisted shopping is among its most common activities.

### OpenAI dissolves its standalone robotics team and moves researchers into its main research group

OpenAI confirmed the reorganization to Fortune, which reported it on November 13. Robotics researchers now report to Chief Research Officer Mark Chen through the main research organization. Applied robotics and hardware staff join the Applied division under President Greg Brockman. A spokesperson described robotics as central to the company's long-term plans and characterized the move as an investment in the field.

Early this year, the company saw video-generation models such as Sora as the most promising route into robotics. It says general-purpose frontier models including GPT-6 Astra have since shown that they can control robots directly. The change was recent enough that OpenAI had registered for booth 208 at this week's CoRL robotics conference in Austin. Fortune's reporter found it empty. The company shut an earlier robotics team in 2020 and rebuilt one in 2024. It has said it is developing its own humanoid robot, with Sam Altman aiming for a first public demonstration in 2027.

Fortune also described pressure to cut spending and concentrate on core products, citing a reported operating loss of $10.9 billion in the latest quarter. Carnegie Mellon's Guanya Shi read the move as a sign that OpenAI no longer sees robotics as a problem requiring its own approach. MIT professor and Walden Robotics CEO Russ Tedrake pointed to an advantage robotics companies retain over AI labs: experience and data from operating machines in factories and other real settings. Today's paper tests what happens when GPT-6 Astra directly drives a robot arm.

[Fortune | November 13, Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/11/13/openai-disbands-robotics-team-as-its-general-models-start-running-robots/)

### Report finds numerical traces consistent with NVIDIA Blackwell chips in six Chinese open-weight models

Will DePue of the nonprofit Unemployment Labs released a report on November 12 examining public weights from DeepSeek, Moonshot AI, MiniMax and Ant Group. It says six models released since April 2026 have numerical features consistent with NVIDIA Blackwell GPUs, while earlier releases from the same companies match the preceding Hopper generation. The six are DeepSeek-V4-Flash, DeepSeek-V4-Pro, Kimi K3, Kimi K3.1-Pro, MiniMax-M3 and Ling-3.1-flash. The report says these Blackwell chips require a US export license for China and that no such license has been reported.

DePue recovered traces of how weights were initialized and looked for hardware and initialization settings that reproduced them. For DeepSeek-V4-Flash and Kimi K3, he confirmed the match on physical Blackwell hardware. The fingerprint concerns initialization. It supports the possibility that training also used Blackwell but cannot establish that. The only alternative he identified involved specially configured AMD hardware, also subject to US export controls. He ruled it out for DeepSeek-V4-Flash and considered it unlikely for the other five. The findings establish neither the chips' location or route of acquisition nor an export-control violation.

TechCrunch reported on November 13 that the chips could have been in data centers overseas. It connected the question to Jensen Huang's departure from NVIDIA this week and US and Singaporean investigations into Singapore-based Megaspeed. TechCrunch cited a November 13 Bloomberg report linking a company associated with Megaspeed executive Alice Huang to the smuggling of restricted NVIDIA chips to a Shanghai data center. This issue did not review Bloomberg's original reporting. TechCrunch also noted the limited obligations US companies currently have to track customers' use of controlled products.

Separately, TechCrunch reported that Richard Neal and Ron Wyden, the ranking Democrats on the House Ways and Means Committee and Senate Finance Committee respectively, wrote to Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick on November 12 asking the department to preserve records about arrangements for NVIDIA chip sales to China. As members of the minority, they can ask questions but cannot compel answers.

### Logician uses Claude to decipher Gödel's 1,009 pages of shorthand and reports hundreds of previously unknown theorems

Since 2017, Finnish logician Jan von Plato has led a University of Helsinki project deciphering 16 workbooks Kurt Gödel kept in the early 1940s. Most of their 1,009 pages use Gabelsberger, a German shorthand already falling out of use at the time. The Guardian reported on November 13 that understanding their mathematical content previously took about a week per page.

In September, von Plato supplied Claude with his transcriptions, six books he had written about Gödel and other secondary literature. He said the model produced a 650-page interpretation in a few days that needed checking page by page. He then asked Claude to extract every theorem for which the notebooks contained a proof, a draft or a proof idea. It returned 663. In tests with the Coq proof assistant, he said about 400 passed as stated, about 200 passed after repairs such as a more precise statement or an added assumption, and about 50 were wrong. He also said dozens of passages anticipated results that became known in the 1970s and 1980s.

One example concerns the independence of the axiom of choice. Paul Cohen received a Fields Medal in 1966 for proving the independence of the continuum hypothesis and the axiom of choice. Gödel said at the time that he had obtained the latter result in the 1940s but could not reconstruct his proof. When von Plato asked Claude to look for it, the model concluded that Gödel had come within one step: he had used an incorrect definition, having used the correct one two years earlier. Von Plato said the reconstructed proof passed Coq's checks.

University of Notre Dame logician Joel David Hamkins had not seen the reconstruction. He told The Guardian it would be historically interesting if it held up, while leaving historians to determine how much to attribute to Gödel and how much to Claude. A passage resembling Cohen's proof would not itself establish that Gödel had the proof at the time. The report did not identify a formal publication of the results.

[The Guardian | November 13, The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/science/2026/nov/13/kurt-godel-notebooks-ai-helps-unlock-hidden-discoveries-man-who-transformed-maths)

### Google Research describes Antigravity's /teamwork workflow for multiple agents

A November 13 Google Research post describes two ways the workflow tackles long tasks. Agents can explore solutions in parallel, then test candidates with tools such as the Lean 4 proof checker or an unmodified software test suite. The system also splits context between steps. Each specialist receives the files and tools it needs, while a coordinator assigns the next step to a fresh agent. Google said internal teams and academic collaborators had used /teamwork with Gemini 4 models in recent weeks on open questions in mathematics, chemistry and systems software engineering. Human experts participated in all of the work. The feature is available in Antigravity. This issue did not check each reported result individually.

## Paper Brief

<!-- item:paper-justrua-gpt-6-robot-use-agents -->
### JustRUA tests which design choices help GPT-6 Astra control a robot arm

**Research question** A robot-use agent (RUA) uses a general-purpose multimodal model and a fixed software harness to direct a robot arm without robot-specific training. Recent systems have improved quickly on the RoboDojo simulation benchmark, but different harnesses, test subsets and simultaneous changes make the improvements hard to attribute. This arXiv preprint (2611.16009 v1, submitted November 11) varies the design choices within a single framework.

**Method** The starting point was the official L3 harness from the GPT-6 Astra leaderboard entry. At each step, the model sees RGB images from the head and both wrists, along with the state of both arms. It specifies a target grasp pose and a planner with no learned component computes a joint trajectory. The model receives no depth, object pose or scoring signal. Instead of the official 2,100 episodes, the authors constructed a 111-episode set covering all 42 tasks, testing four layouts each for 23 tasks with variable outcomes.

**Reported results** On that set the score rose from 29.7 to 50.3 without a learned policy, a self-improvement loop or task-specific tuning. The authors retained three groups of changes: keeping the full episode history and stopping each action early, adding robot readings and predicted depth to observations, and allowing the model to write code to recover 3D coordinates from pixels. Scene geometry helped most in their analysis. Adding depth maps reduced grasps at empty space from 41 to 16, even though Depth Pro estimated depth from RGB with errors of tens of centimeters. They discarded alternative action interfaces, precomputed affordances and experience reuse. Experience reuse drew on the scored tasks themselves, so the authors excluded it regardless of its score.

**Limitations** All experiments used one model and one simulation benchmark. Each configuration ran once on a test set selected using the authors' own test records. The scores are not official leaderboard results. With the final configuration, 21 of the 42 tasks still scored below 50 on their first episode. Most causes of failure varied by task.

**Why read it today?** OpenAI reportedly dissolved its standalone robotics team on the premise that a general-purpose model can directly control a robot. JustRUA puts numbers on what such a model can do in simulation with a simple harness and where it still struggles.

[arXiv | Submitted November 11. Announced November 13 on arXiv (v1 preprint).](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16009)

### Jason Ma on why each increase in the size of a robot deployment reveals different problems

**Speaker** Jason Ma is co-founder and chief scientist of Dyna Robotics and previously conducted research at UC Berkeley. The Concourse introduction describes him looking back on 300 days deploying dual-arm robots at customer sites including commercial laundries.

**Program** SV101 is a Chinese technology interview video podcast. The episode was released on October 19, 2026, and added to Concourse on October 21.

**Argument** In Concourse's excerpts, Ma says a robot genuinely doing its job ought to look boring. He treats real deployments as the measure of a system and attributes failures to the model and wider system rather than a lack of legs or hands. In one excerpt checked against the original recording, he describes how deploying one, 10, 100 or 1,000 robots exposes different problems at each scale. He compares the current stage of embodied intelligence to the period between GPT-2 and GPT-3.

**Connection to this issue** OpenAI's reorganization bets on direct control by general-purpose models. JustRUA measures that possibility in simulation. Ma describes engineering difficulties that emerge with fleet size, echoing Tedrake's point in Fortune. Together they raise the question of how much field experience a general model can replace. This is the digest's analysis of an interview recorded before today's news.

<!-- item:discourse-terence-tao-proof-abundance -->
### Terence Tao on why a growing supply of machine proofs has yet to yield a matching gain in understanding

**Speaker** Fields Medalist Terence Tao is a professor at UCLA.

**Program** Numberphile is a mathematics video interview series. This episode appeared on October 15, 2026, and Concourse added it on October 21.

**Argument** In excerpts drawn from automatic captions, Tao describes mathematics moving from scarce proofs to abundant ones. Machines can produce so many proofs that understanding struggles to keep pace. He compares it to a shift from scarce, nourishing food to widely available synthetic food that is edible but less satisfying. He also sees mathematics moving away from an era of individual heroes toward the larger collaborations familiar in other fields. He worries that AI systems are using up existing questions without replenishing them.

**Connection to this issue** The Gödel notebooks offer a case in point. Claude delivered 650 pages of interpretation in days. Von Plato says each page still needed checking, and he tested the extracted theorems with a proof assistant. Hamkins points out the distance between finding a similar argument in a notebook and establishing that its author had a proof. Once reading a thousand pages costs less, understanding and verification become the scarce resources. This is the digest's interpretation. The interview predates today's news.
