Jensen Huang resigns as NVIDIA CEO and director, with CFO Colette Kress named interim CEO

NVIDIA's board announced on November 12 that Jensen Huang had resigned as president, chief executive and director, effective immediately. Executive Vice President and CFO Colette Kress becomes interim CEO while retaining her finance role until a permanent successor is appointed. The board will consider internal and external candidates. Kress has served as CFO since joining NVIDIA in September 2013. Harvey C. Jones was also appointed the company's first board chair. The announcement said operations, customer commitments and the product roadmap were expected to continue unaffected, with fiscal 2027 third-quarter results still scheduled for November 17.

WIRED reported that the resignation came four days after NVIDIA announced an internal investigation. On November 8, the board announced an inquiry into Huang's relationship with Alice Huang. The New York Times and Financial Times had reported on November 6 that she had been recruited by China's Ministry of State Security to gather information targeting him. The Chinese government denied those allegations. On November 11, The New York Times additionally reported an alleged relationship with a direct report. One person familiar with the matter told WIRED that the relationship with a subordinate and Huang's business dealings with Singapore-based Megaspeed, where Alice Huang had held a senior position, may have weighed more heavily on the board than the espionage allegations. That assessment rests on a single anonymous source.

The Verge published two internal emails. Jones informed staff of the resignation and appointments. In her first email to employees, Kress acknowledged how difficult the period had been and said an all-hands meeting would take place on November 18. Separately, TechCrunch, citing Bloomberg and NBC News, reported that House Foreign Affairs Committee chair Brian Mast said Huang had agreed to testify before Congress before Thanksgiving. The testimony would be private and was expected to be recorded and transcribed.

Google releases Gemini 4 Hydrogen, replacing 3.8 Flash and becoming Search AI Mode's default

Google releases Gemini 4 Hydrogen, replacing 3.8 Flash and becoming Search AI Mode's default

Google released Gemini 4 Hydrogen on November 12. This fast model in the series targets everyday coding and agent tasks, succeeding Gemini 3.8 Flash. It became available that day across Google AI Studio tiers, the Gemini API, Gemini Enterprise, the Gemini app and Google Search, and in Google Antigravity for paying Google AI Pro and Ultra subscribers. Pricing remains flat across its one-million-token context window, at $1.50 per million input tokens and $7.50 per million output tokens.

Google's comparisons draw on its own tests and third-party leaderboards. Hydrogen scores 51 at its default thinking level on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, versus 50 for Gemini 4 Argon, reaching 54 at the highest xhigh setting. Google says the default setting completes tasks 2.5 times faster than Gemini 3.8 Flash, while Terminal-Bench 4.1 performance rises from 18.2% to 57%. The four thinking levels are low, medium, high, which is the default, and the new xhigh. Google also reports fewer cases of agents exceeding authority, taking shortcuts or bypassing permission boundaries than with 3.8 Flash. Those safety results come from internal evaluations.

Google Antigravity also introduced Orchestrator, which switches between Gemini 4 Carbon and Hydrogen within a session. Carbon might first inspect dependencies in read-only mode and formulate a plan, leaving Hydrogen to edit code and run tests, then take over again if problems arise. Google says Orchestrator approaches Carbon-only task success on the benchmark while using 24% less quota and running 19% faster on average. The Google blog identifies that benchmark as version 4.0, while the Antigravity blog says 4.1.

Google Search VP Robby Stein described this as Search's largest model upgrade to date. According to The Verge, Hydrogen is now AI Mode's default and handles the hardest AI Overviews queries. Google says it can interpret multipart questions and difficult misspellings, search subtopics in parallel, and return a broader range of links alongside more images, comparison tables and other visuals. This issue's paper retrospective examines the quality of AI Overviews citations.

Study finds fake or defunct shopping websites in up to 15% of chatbot responses

Study finds fake or defunct shopping websites in up to 15% of chatbot responses

The Verge exclusively reported on November 12 on a test by intellectual-property protection company Podqi. Researchers asked ChatGPT, Gemini and Perplexity shopping questions about 151 large and small brands, such as where to buy a brand or find its regional websites. Two rounds in September produced 6,795 responses.

The study classified three types of sites as risky, covering fake retailers or counterfeit sellers, former brand domains re-registered by others, and inactive domains that someone else could buy and exploit. All three products surfaced such sites. They appeared in 15% of Perplexity responses, 8% of ChatGPT responses and 4% of Gemini responses. Appearance included recommendations, unqualified listings and warnings to exercise caution. ChatGPT warned users most often, at 24%. Smaller brands were more exposed, with risky sites appearing about half as frequently for large companies. Questions about regional websites were especially problematic, sometimes producing fake regional sites for brands with no regional website at all.

Several qualifications matter. Podqi sells brand-protection services, and tested brand Madhappy is a client. Researchers designed the prompts, which were not real user conversations. All three companies responded. Google welcomed Gemini's relative performance and said it displays source links for checking. OpenAI said it was improving safeguards to reduce suspicious shopping results. Perplexity said it never claims complete accuracy. Podqi CEO Trevor West advised checking a site's provenance before entering personal or credit-card information.

Nebius plans $40–45 billion of 2027 spending, largely financed through customer prepayments and contracts

Nebius plans $40–45 billion of 2027 spending, largely financed through customer prepayments and contracts

TechCrunch reported that on its November 12 third-quarter earnings call, neocloud provider Nebius outlined capital expenditure of $40–45 billion in 2027, far above Wall Street expectations. The company expects customer prepayments and financing secured against investment-grade customer contracts to cover up to 85% of that amount. Its CFO specifically cited Microsoft and Meta contracts as financing collateral. This resembles the CoreWeave approach covered in yesterday's issue.

Delivery is the risk. Microsoft is one of Nebius's largest customers, with a contract worth up to $19.4 billion. The associated Vineland, New Jersey, data center is already months behind schedule. Only a little over half of Microsoft's capacity is now expected by year-end, with the rest pushed to the second quarter of 2027. Nebius said third-party site operator DataOne had retired temporary generators sooner than expected and was switching to fuel cells. TechCrunch added that an August investigation found at least 45 natural-gas generators operating without permits, leading New Jersey regulators to impose a $1.1 million fine. According to Nebius's contract summary, late delivery without replacement capacity lets Microsoft terminate affected services after a grace period, and the contract includes delay penalties. Nebius said Microsoft had participated in planning but did not say whether it agreed to the new dates.

Third-quarter revenue was $981 million, up 571% year over year, with roughly one-third coming from a one-off payment. Nebius cut its 2026 revenue forecast by $200 million to $3 billion, citing third-party site delays. Shares fell 17% that day. CEO Arkady Volozh said on the call that construction on this scale was difficult, that delays were widespread across the industry and that Nebius was also affected.

Fireworks AI confirms theft of customer billing information and some datasets, affecting about 1% of Notion customers

Fireworks AI confirms theft of customer billing information and some datasets, affecting about 1% of Notion customers

TechCrunch reported on November 12 that model-hosting platform Fireworks AI had confirmed a cyberattack. Its security notice said attackers first compromised its cloud provider, then entered a Fireworks development system and stole customer billing information and some datasets. Fireworks says it serves tens of thousands of customers, including legal-tech company Harvey and Cursor.

Notion is one confirmed affected customer. It disclosed that the incident affected roughly 1% of its customers. The stolen material comprised chat transcripts from Notion AI customers using Insights, including all model inputs and outputs within those conversations. Much of the data could not be tied to specific customers. Notion said it disconnected Fireworks on November 3 and deleted the data stored in its systems.

Fireworks believes the attack was financially motivated, aimed at stealing access to large language models and reselling usage to unauthorized third parties. It was the company's second security incident in two months. The incident illustrates a structural issue, as outsourcing model calls can also place user conversations in an upstream provider's systems.

Related-paper retrospective estimates that only about 57% of cited health claims in Google AI Overviews are supported by their sources2026-10-29 arXiv v1 submission, historical retrospectivearXiv 2610.39339 v1 HTML, abstract, introduction, sections 2.1–2.3, 3.1 and table A5, 3.2–3.4 and table 3, discussion limitations and appendix on automated coding reviewed. Sections 2.4–2.7 and robustness section 3.5 were not reviewed paragraph by paragraph. Only v1 exists.

Why read it today Google is bringing Gemini 4 Hydrogen into Search and promises a broader range of links, while Podqi's test finds chatbots pointing shoppers toward fake sites. Both raise questions about the reliability of AI-generated links. This Northwestern University preprint, submitted to arXiv on October 29 as v1 and not peer-reviewed, audits AI Overviews citations. It is historical research outside this issue's news window. Data were collected in September, before the model upgrade, so the study cannot evaluate Hydrogen.

Method The researchers selected 3,611 English health questions from OpenAI's HealthBench. Using US-English settings, they submitted each to Google through SerpAPI on September 16–18, 2026, collecting AI Overviews, citations and the top five conventional search results. The questions were designed to evaluate conversational models and were longer than typical search queries.

Results AI Overviews appeared for 96.4% of questions. Only 44.4% of the top five conventional results were also cited in an Overview. Overviews cited more commercial health sites and fewer academic medical institutions and government sites. They scored below conventional results on four of six website-credibility measures, though effect sizes were small, with a maximum dz of 0.19. Across 3,474 Overviews, 24,060 claims included 12,175 with checkable citations. Automated checker HallOumi judged 47.1% supported by the cited webpage. Adjustment using human annotations raised the estimate to 56.7%, with a 95% confidence interval of 45.3% to 68.6%. Another 20.8% of claims were factual statements without any citation. Against physician-written rubrics, scored automatically by GPT-4.1, Overviews earned an average of 38.2% of available points, including 54.2% for accuracy and 21.6% for completeness. About 90% of lost points reflected omissions. Of 2,065 questions where physicians identified a particular error or potentially harmful statement, 22.2% of Overviews contained that content. For 413 questions requiring urgent medical attention, 94.9% gave advice with appropriate urgency.

Limits Models performed scoring and citation verification. Questions came from a benchmark rather than real search logs. The audit covered US English over three days. The 11.7% of cited pages that could not be read were excluded. The authors also found no relationship between answer accuracy and whether claims had citation support.

IBM Granite's LLMON separates instructions from data in a markup language for language modelsnot_verified

IBM's Granite team introduced LLMON, short for LLM Object Notation and pronounced lemon, in a November 12 Hugging Face blog post. It released two experimental preview models, granite-4.1-3b-LLMON-preview and granite-4.1-8b-LLMON-preview, plus a GitHub repository with a toolkit, example workflows and templates.

Model context mixes system prompts, conversation history, retrieved documents, tool results and output requirements. These structures are either implicit or expressed in JSON, which was designed for parsers. A model encounters the same quotation-mark token in a document and at the end of a field. LLMON uses XML-like markup with reserved special tokens as structural delimiters. Each component has a tag and unique ID, while an exec block specifies which instruction to execute, which data it applies to and the applicable constraints.

The team says explicit separation makes LLMON models ignore most indirect prompt injections hidden in user data, with attack success approaching zero on some benchmarks while preserving general capabilities relative to Granite 4.1 Instruct. The post names no specific benchmark or numerical result for that claim, which remains the authors' account. It also provides lossless conversion between JSON and LLMON, with native support in IBM's Mellea. Both models are experimental previews. This issue did not verify their licenses or run them.
