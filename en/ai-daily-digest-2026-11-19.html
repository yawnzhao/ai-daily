Gemini 4 Neon puts advanced capabilities within reach for routine use

Google DeepMind released Gemini 4 Neon, a model positioned between Gemini 4 Carbon and Gemini 4 Hydrogen for complex coding, analytical reasoning and enterprise workflows. Google reports that Neon scored 61 and ranked third overall on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index at the xhigh reasoning setting. Pricing is $3 per million input tokens and $15 per million output tokens, with a context window of one million tokens.

Neon is available on the paid Gemini API tier and in Gemini for business, to paying Pro and Ultra subscribers in Google Antigravity, and to Pro and Ultra subscribers in the Gemini app. The Gemini app's task feature can also run on Neon. A cybersecurity version will be made available to trusted defenders through the Fairwind program over the next few days.

The announcement draws primarily on third-party leaderboards cited by Google and Google's own evaluations. Google reports that Neon costs 32% less per task than Carbon at the default high setting. Developers can also compare which reasoning setting is enough for their own tasks and whether context compaction holds up over long sessions. Google says Neon maintains abuse and prompt-injection safeguards comparable to Carbon's.

Models

Microsoft introduces seven MAI models, with an emphasis on cost per task

At Ignite, Microsoft AI announced MAI Cyber 2 Flash, MAI Code 2, MAI Code 2 Flash, MAI Image 2.7, MAI Realtime 1, Dragon Copilot Transcribe based on MAI Transcribe 2, and Decision 1.1 for model routing. Its second-generation AI accelerator, Maia 200, is also in production.

Microsoft's comparison focuses on price. Across four benchmarks for everyday enterprise coding, Microsoft reports about 98% of GPT-6.1 Sol’s performance for MAI Code 2, at about 40% of the price. Microsoft says its coding models have yet to be tuned for fundamental scientific research or very long-running agent tasks. In GitHub Copilot, developers can now try MAI Code 2.

MAI Licensed Weights lets enterprises run MAI models in their own Azure environments, adapt them using their own data and manage access themselves. The figures come from Microsoft's benchmark and pricing comparisons. The announcement appeared on November 17 and is included under this issue's 48-hour catch-up window.

Industry and finance

NVIDIA has stopped shipping licensed H200 chips to China

The Verge reports that interim CEO Colette Kress said on Tuesday's earnings call that NVIDIA's guidance includes no licensed Hopper data-center products and that the company is no longer shipping those products to China. She gave no reason and did not say whether NVIDIA would pursue other business there.

NVIDIA's fiscal third-quarter results, released the same day, put quarterly revenue at $115.8 billion, including $107.6 billion from data centers. The company forecasts about $130 billion in fiscal fourth-quarter revenue and assumes no data-center compute revenue from China.

The U.S. government had previously approved H200 sales to China. The report also notes concerns about how thoroughly approved Chinese companies were vetted. Many chips remained in Hong Kong because import clearance had yet to come through. Export licenses, import approvals and NVIDIA's own business decisions each affect whether a shipment reaches the customer.

In Project Genie, users can change their worlds as they explore

Eligible Google AI Ultra subscribers are getting access to the Genie 4 world model through an updated Project Genie. Users can keep entering prompts while exploring a world to change the weather, add characters or steer the story. The model also generates audio that changes with the scene.

02
Coding agents can send their code to an external sandbox

Simon Willison and AI research lab Prime Radiant describe an external sandbox arrangement. The agent stays outside while the code it needs to run executes in a separate sandbox. The authors released three sandbox server implementations based on v86 in WebAssembly, Apple Containers and Microsoft's Quicksand library. They expose the same local interface for agents such as Pi, Claude Code and Codex.

An optional HTTP/HTTPS allowlist proxy limits which hosts the code can reach. Credential substitution lets code call authenticated interfaces without handing secrets to the agent. The authors want to make local models easier to try while reducing the risk of accidental damage or prompt injection.

03
Banks spell out customer liability when AI agents get account access

TechCrunch reports that Citi updated its online user agreement this week. Customers who grant a third-party AI agent access to a bank account are responsible for actions taken through that agent. If the AI tool leaks credentials or is compromised, the customer also bears responsibility for what attackers do with the account. A Citi spokesperson told TechCrunch that the bank wants customers to understand the risks of sharing bank credentials or granting these tools access.

According to the report, JPMorgan Chase and Bank of America announced similar policies last month. PNC changed its terms on the day of the report, and Citizens issued a similar notice this week. Citi's terms say that using its own AI agents does not create extra liability. The report also notes that Anthropic's personal finance tools launched this month as read-only tools that cannot move or manage funds.

04
Disney writes the lines for its Star Wars robots and lets AI choose when to use them

The Verge reports that Walt Disney Imagineering demonstrated three animatronic robots modeled on Anzellan characters from Star Wars. Multiple AI models running in the AWS cloud allow them to respond to visitors in real time. Concealed cameras and microphones pick up movement and speech. A fast decision model handles immediate responses such as eye contact, while a larger model plans longer interactions.

Imagineers wrote each action and line in advance. AI selects and triggers those responses during the interaction. The three prototypes are due to make their public debut at AWS re:Invent this month and could eventually appear in Disney parks.

AI advances quickly on tasks with checkable answers. What about judgment calls?

Andrew Ng founded DeepLearning.AI, co-founded Coursera and teaches as an adjunct professor at Stanford University. He was a founding lead of Google Brain and served as vice president and chief scientist at Baidu. His work in machine learning research and online education informs how he looks at AI deployment.

Stanford GSB released this fireside conversation on October 7, 2026, in connection with the World Bank's World Development Report 2026. It covers how AI can support economic development, education and entrepreneurship.

At 00:32:08–00:34:16, Ng observes that AI has progressed quickly in tasks whose answers can be checked, such as programming or mathematical proofs. Many jobs also require judgment on questions without a single correct answer, such as which product to build. He argues that the gap between progress on the first kind of task and the ability to make those judgments may be larger than commonly assumed. The observation leaves open how AI might improve on open-ended tasks. At 00:09:04–00:11:14, Ng also describes open-weight models as a way for people, companies and research institutions with fewer resources to gain access to AI. For countries concerned about losing access to external services, he sees joint support for open models as a relatively economical option. Computing, maintenance and security still carry costs.

This issue reads the conversation alongside the Neon and MAI announcements, which rely heavily on benchmarks with scorable answers. Benchmark scores can track progress on those tasks. Users still have to decide which model to use and where in the workflow. This account paraphrases the relevant auto-captioned segments compiled by Concourse. The original audio was not reviewed for this item.

Research Briefs

1 paper
01In AI-assisted mathematics, how students' questions change matters more than how often they ask
ResearchOctober 28 preprint, revisited from Paper-RadarSections 4.1–4.3, 5.1–5.4, 6 and 7, and Table 2 on the sample, exercise, coding, regression and limitations.

Cheaper, more readily available AI assistants are entering schools and workplaces. How people use them may affect what they can do once the tool is removed. A preprint submitted on October 28 by Rania Abdelghani, Peter Kaiser and Kou Murayama analyzes conversations from 112 ninth-grade students at three academic-track secondary schools in Baden-Württemberg, Germany. The students used the general-purpose model Mistral for an open-ended mathematics modeling exercise lasting about 40 minutes.

Section 5.2 reports that 81.5% of 880 valid student utterances were help requests, with relatively little explicit planning, monitoring or evaluation. About half of those requests sought to understand concepts or steps. The others sought answers or confirmation. Section 5.3 reports that, on average, students shifted toward asking for answers or confirmation as the conversation went on.

The hierarchical regression in Section 5.4 and Table 2 covered 89 students with complete data. After accounting for pretest scores, students whose requests shifted toward understanding performed better on a posttest without AI. Static measures such as time spent chatting and the overall mix of help requests added no detectable explanatory power.

Section 7 describes the limits of the observational study. It covers a single short class session, one model and one grade level, and cannot establish causation. The posttest immediately followed the exercise and does not measure long-term retention. The paper was selected from Paper-Radar for a related retrospective and retains its October submission date.

Rumble-2-Flash is an open 0.5B speech recognition modelApache-2.0

Reson8 released Rumble-2-Flash under Apache 2.0. The 500-million-parameter speech recognition model supports more than 40 languages, uses the Qwen3-ASR architecture and runs in Transformers and vLLM without custom code.

The publisher says it used on-policy distillation from its larger production model, Rumble-2. It reports an average error rate of 6.96% across nine languages on the multilingual track of the Open ASR Leaderboard. On AppTek's 302-hour call-center benchmark, the publisher reports results close to those of the 30-billion-parameter Nemotron 3.5 Transcribe. This issue checked the release post and did not run the model locally.
