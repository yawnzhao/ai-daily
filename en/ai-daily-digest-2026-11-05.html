Anthropic introduces FlashProve to verify which model actually produced an answer

Anthropic introduces FlashProve to verify which model actually produced an answer

Researchers from Anthropic, EPFL, MIT, and other institutions published an article on provable inference, introducing a system called FlashProve. The problem is specific. Users cannot currently verify whether an answer came from the model the developer claims, whether a routing service quietly substituted another model to save money, or whether an attacker altered the output token stream. The provider first makes a cryptographic commitment to a model, then supplies a proof with each answer. Users can verify it quickly on their own devices without obtaining the weights or running the model. TechCrunch compares it to an encrypted email signature and notes that it could prevent companies from secretly substituting cheaper models to cut costs or more expensive models to inflate evaluation scores.

The article describes a zero-knowledge succinct proof called a zkSNARK, with system improvements developed with Claude's help. Under four-bit quantization and integer arithmetic during prefill, the authors report that a single H100 can generate proofs for about 110 tokens per second for the roughly 400-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts model Llama 4 Maverick. A request of 1,024 tokens produces a proof of about 19 MB. GPU time is about 70 to 95 times that needed to run the model directly with vLLM, while remaining orders of magnitude faster than earlier open-source systems. To make Claude's complex code auditable, the team wrote top-level security statements and proofs in the formal verification language Lean. The technical report, protocol specification, prover, fast C++ verifier, and Lean verifier are available under Apache 2.0.

The limitations are explicit. This is a research prototype, and the authors do not guarantee complete agreement among the fast executable code, the paper, and the Lean protocol. It hides weights but does not aim to hide the architecture, and it assumes a mixture-of-experts model. TechCrunch reports that there are currently no plans to deploy it at Anthropic or other labs. The authors also suggest that proofs could help independent evaluators establish that they are testing the production model and could help verify compliance with commitments about the pace of AI development.

U.S. midterm votes restrict data centers, while several candidates supporting moratoriums win

U.S. midterm votes restrict data centers, while several candidates supporting moratoriums win

The November 3 midterms made data centers an immediate electoral cost for the AI industry. Citing Redbud Consulting, The Verge reports 45 local votes directly concerning data centers, with 33 producing restrictions by Wednesday morning. Wells, Maine, approved a 180-day moratorium on large data centers, retroactive to mid-June 2026. Several Ohio cities banned facilities above 25 megawatts. El Dorado, Kansas, banned data centers with peak loads above 10 megawatts for ten years. Using a different count, 404 Media reports that opponents won at least 38 of more than 40 votes across 13 states, including outright bans in six cities and power limits in twelve. NBC News's Election Day polling found 76 percent opposed a data center in their community. WIRED, citing the same organization, says almost a quarter considered data centers crucial to their vote.

In Texas, Democrat Gina Hinojosa defeated Governor Greg Abbott, who was seeking a fourth term. She supports a statewide data-center moratorium. Georgia elected Keisha Lance Bottoms, who also supports a moratorium. Both states are major data-center hubs, and WIRED notes that hundreds of planned projects could be affected. Ohio's Sherrod Brown, who portrayed his opponent as a spokesman for data centers, flipped a Senate seat for the Democrats.

The Verge cautions that a candidate's victory cannot easily be attributed to data centers alone. Candidates favoring more moderate limits also won, including Republican Byron Donalds in Florida, who opposed a blanket ban. Nearly every candidate, however, had to address the issue. Local voters have joined chips and electricity among the constraints on AI companies' compute expansion.

The largest pro-AI super PAC network struggles in contested races as OpenAI looks toward 2028

The largest pro-AI super PAC network struggles in contested races as OpenAI looks toward 2028

WIRED reviewed Leading the Future, the largest U.S. pro-AI super PAC network. Launched in August 2025 with a claimed funding pool of about $100 million, its backers included a16z, Palantir co-founder Joe Lonsdale, and OpenAI's Greg Brockman. It ended the election with more than $20 million remaining. Dozens of congressional candidates it supported won, but WIRED argues that most funding went to safe bets. It received no new donations in the second or third quarters, and most of its bets in the few competitive districts failed. Its American Mission group spent more than $2 million on South Carolina's Darline Graham, whose support for data-center expansion earned her a data-center nickname from opponents. She lost with 46.5 percent of the vote. In the primaries, the network spent millions attacking AI-safety advocate Alex Bores, a New York state legislator. Public First, a safety-focused network heavily funded by Anthropic, spent $15 million supporting him. Bores finished second in a multi-candidate primary.

After the election, OpenAI chief global affairs officer Chris Lehane published an article comparing AI's potential economic transformation to Roosevelt's New Deal. He called for a third path that lets ordinary people share the benefits, outlining five policy priorities covering worker protection, education, AI safety, and data centers. TechCrunch quotes AI Now Institute's Kate Brennan describing a marked shift from last year's emphasis on acceleration and less regulation, while noting that many proposals still promote wider adoption and infrastructure. She criticizes a private company's use of the New Deal to lobby for its own interests as historically careless at best and harmful at worst. TechCrunch also observes that AI itself did not dominate the midterms. Data centers were the issue that resonated across party lines.

Route Anything studies how to choose the model or strategy for each taskarXiv v1 submitted November 3, 2026 at 17:56 UTC; 48-hour catch-upThe arXiv abstract page for v1. The full paper was not read.

This paper, submitted to arXiv on November 3, examines routing among models and strategies. A router selects a model, tool, or policy for each task to balance quality and cost. The authors build an evaluation matrix spanning 25 benchmarks for language understanding, image, video and speech generation, coding and office agents, and robotic manipulation, with 374,401 scored runs across 452 candidate configurations. The abstract reports that an ideal router choosing the best candidate for each input beats the best single candidate by 5.4 points. A trained router averages a 0.3-point improvement on 16 benchmarks, with gains of 2.1 points in video generation and 1.1 in robotics while using 24 percent less policy time. Average efficiency is 8.82 times the reference model's. Read alongside FlashProve, it raises a related question. As routing becomes more common, verifying which model answered becomes more consequential.
