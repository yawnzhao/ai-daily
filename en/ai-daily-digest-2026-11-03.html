Copyright

The New York Times and other publishers sue Brave, alleging it scraped news, sold it to AI companies, and helped users past paywalls

On November 2, the New York Times sued the browser company Brave in federal court in Manhattan. The same day, the New York Daily News, the Chicago Tribune, and the Orlando Sentinel filed similar suits. The Verge and TechCrunch report that the plaintiffs allege Brave used an unidentified crawler to build an index of more than 30 billion web pages, which publishers could not block, and then sold live web content from that Brave index to AI companies through a paid interface, for training models, answering users, or both. The complaint names Anthropic, Perplexity, Mistral, xAI, and Cohere as customers. The suits also target Brave's paywall-bypass feature, and the practice of blocking publishers' ads while selling Brave's own.

In the complaint, the New York Times casts Brave as a supplier and distributor for AI companies, both a thief and a fence. A Times spokesperson said Brave ignored repeated requests to stop the infringement. A lawyer for the other three papers said stolen goods do not become lawful because they were bought secondhand. A Perplexity spokesperson replied that the Times pays its lawyers too much and its journalists too little. Brave and the other named companies had not responded at the time. The Times has already sued OpenAI and Perplexity. What is different here is that the target moves from the model companies to the middle layer that supplies web data to AI. The Times seeks an injunction and damages. The above is the plaintiffs' allegations. The case has not been tried. The quoted sense follows the Chinese edition's account of the complaints and the reported statements.

Deepfakes

A WIRED investigation: more than 400 apps dressed up as pet portraits or language tutors quietly offer sexually exploitative deepfakes

WIRED published an investigation on November 2. A network of more than 400 linked domains and apps presents itself as ordinary tools, such as pet portraits, language tutoring, or an AI fitness coach. Under certain conditions it quietly loads a web interface that lets a user upload someone else's photo and generate explicit deepfake images and video, including templates for violent sexual assault. The trail started with researchers following thousands of explicit deepfake-video ads on Meta's Facebook and Instagram. The ads pointed people to disguised apps in the Apple and Google stores, and the apps could be paid for with Apple Pay and Google Pay.

Paul Bouchaud, a researcher at the nonprofit AI Forensics, reverse-engineered the app code. After each download, the app sends the referrer, the language, and a device identifier to a server, and the server decides whether to show the ordinary version or the explicit one. Arriving from a Meta ad appears to be one of the triggers. A researcher at the security firm Infoblox found nearly 500 related domains running the same code and pointing at one another. At the peak, about 30 new apps and sites were registered each week. In September, 27 were still in the Google store, with more than 350,000 downloads in total. DiffAI alone had more than 100,000. WIRED says the apps and domains appear to be run by developers in China and by KeLing Technology Limited, a company registered in Hong Kong. They did not comment. After WIRED asked, Apple removed 19 apps, including Viefur, and said the apps had passed review and only later swapped in sexual content. Google did not respond, but it had already removed most of the related apps before being contacted. A review only at listing time is a weak check on an app that changes what it serves after it is approved.

Energy

OpenAI announces the Grid Protectors Program: a new Georgia compute campus would give up as much as 1,000 megawatts in an emergency

On November 2, OpenAI announced the Grid Protectors Program. From the summary on its site, the program is meant to work with power companies so that future compute campuses help keep the grid reliable and protect consumers' bills. The Verge reports that the first pilot is a data center still to be built in Effingham County, Georgia. In extreme weather or an emergency, the campus would give up as much as 1,000 megawatts. OpenAI says that is enough to run air conditioning for about 700,000 homes. Its reason is that electricity demand rises and falls with the season, while a compute campus needs power all year. With the right agreement, that steady demand can spread the grid's fixed costs over more electricity sold, and the campus can then cut demand at the peak.

The announcement lands in a wave of opposition to data centers across the United States. The Verge notes that Tennessee, Florida, Oklahoma, Oregon, and other states have passed laws so AI companies cannot shift energy costs onto residents, and some places have pauses. Yesterday this digest reported that OpenAI was considering renaming data centers "AI compute campuses". This program is the first concrete promise attached to that new name. For now it is a pilot at a single campus. What agreement is required to give power back, and who decides the trigger, depends on later arrangements with the power company. The OpenAI site itself hit a Cloudflare check in the browser and could not be opened. The detail follows The Verge.

Musk confirms a tighter Grok Bot allowance: the service cost is unsustainably high relative to revenue

TechCrunch reported on November 2 that over Halloween weekend some Grok Bot users woke to find their weekly allowance already spent, even if they had not used the agent overnight. On X, some said usage jumped from 50 percent to 100 percent. On November 1, SpaceX chief executive Elon Musk, replying to a user, confirmed it was not a malfunction. The contrast is that Grok Bot has been treated as a success story for the newly listed SpaceX. The same day, the SpaceXSI site posted about an internal Data Bot. The data-science team wrote metric definitions, experiment analysis, dashboards, and data-engineering rules as skills, so other teams can ask it questions. The company estimates it took on about 70 hours of work last week that would otherwise have fallen to the data team. The more an agent can do, the more compute it burns. How the allowance is set, and how plainly that is told to users, is becoming a core question for a subscription product. Musk's X post was not opened on its own. Engineering

Microsoft's write-up of Copilot Cowork: nine engineers merged 1,211 pull requests in a day, by writing the spec first and reviewing in layers

On November 2, Microsoft published an engineering-blog post on how it built Copilot Cowork, an agent layer that carries out multi-step workflows for a user. The team says it took about four and a half months from an empty repository to a global launch on June 16, and that later, while the product was running, the runtime moved from Python to Rust. Most of the code was written by agents. On October 21, nine engineers merged 1,211 pull requests into the main backend branch. Monthly merged pull requests rose from 257 in January to 2,754 in May.

The lessons in the post are less about how strong the model is than about how people draw a boundary around the agent. The team's rule is that code does not get written before a Markdown spec, because a vague request only comes back as code that looks plausible and does the wrong thing. Each agent does one task in its own copy of the repository. The model runs in a container that does not hold user credentials. Identity, policy, approval, and tool calls stay with a trusted host outside the container. On review, several engineers each built a review bot and set the bots to compete. The median first-pass review of a recently merged pull request was about four and a half minutes. The post also says that once a set of review agents all missed a problem, and a person found four blocking issues in the same change, so a critical change keeps at least one human check. The team also found that a check meant to limit instruction-file size had already gone over the limit and still showed as passing. This is the Microsoft team's own account. A pull-request count is not a measure of delivered value.

04
Claude's platform opens a public test of message threads, so a long-running agent does not resend the whole history each turn

On November 2, Anthropic announced a public test of message threads on the Claude platform. A coding agent could previously send a hundred or more requests in one session, and each request resent every earlier message and tool result. With message threads, a later request sends only the new content and the ID of the previous response. Anthropic says that after the feature was on by default for most Claude Code users, the median wait for the first token on requests of 500,000 to 1 million tokens fell by about 35 percent. A thread is encrypted in memory, not written to disk, and expires five minutes after the last request. Claude still reads the full conversation on every turn. Token use does not change. What is saved is waiting time, not the bill.

Gabriel Martinez: generation is cheap, and review is expensive

Gabriel Martinez is an engineering manager at G2i. This talk is from AI Engineer, a conference and talk channel for people who practice applying AI. The Concourse record dates it to October 9, 2026. What follows is a paraphrase of the editorial guide and timestamped automatic-caption excerpts on the Concourse page, not a verbatim quotation.

His central point is that generation is cheap and evaluation is expensive. If AI produces more than people can evaluate, it has not sped the team up. It has made a bottleneck. He warns that measuring only output pushes engineers to loosen their care for the long-term health of the code in order to merge faster. He argues for keeping changes small and reviewable, and for clear ownership, and he thinks sixty years of software-engineering experience have not been voided by AI. This is a talk from three weeks ago, not a response to the Microsoft post. The Microsoft team, chasing a thousand pull requests a day while still writing the spec first and keeping a human check on critical changes, is a useful contrast with the problem he raises.

Why read it today: the deepfake-app network in the WIRED investigation is not only a problem at the point of generation. It is also a question of how fast a platform acts after a victim reports it. This audit, published in the Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction, ran a controlled experiment. Its DOI is 10.1145/3816990. The Paper-Radar record date is September 23, 2026. It is not a new paper from today. Researchers uploaded 50 AI-generated nude images to X, formerly Twitter. Half were reported through the platform's channel for non-consensual nude content. The contrast is sharp. Every image reported as copyright was removed within 25 hours, a removal rate of 100 percent. Of the images reported as non-consensual, none had been removed after more than three weeks, a removal rate of 0 percent. The authors call for specific legislation on non-consensual intimate images, to set a platform's duty to remove them, and they discuss the ethics of running this kind of audit on a social platform. The study covers one platform and 50 images. The result does not transfer directly to every platform. The reading scope is the abstract. LongCat-2.5 is announced: a 1.6-trillion-parameter native multimodal model, with the weight pages still marked as coming soonNone

In a technical blog post dated November 1, the LongCat team introduces LongCat-2.5, a native multimodal model aimed at long-running agent tasks. It has 1.6 trillion parameters in total, and about 48 billion parameters active per token. On top of LongCat-2.0 it strengthens visual understanding and agent programming. Images are represented as multi-level discrete tokens, so the backbone handles images and text in one token format. Long-sequence training includes more long-horizon traces. Large-scale reinforcement learning trains end-to-end engineering skills such as building a full project, and real agent failures are turned into training tasks that can be reproduced and checked. The blog also says the team optimized long-context training and W4A4 inference for domestic AI accelerators.

The blog compares it with GPT-5.5, GPT-5.6-Sol, Claude-Opus-4.8, and Claude-Opus-5. Those are the team's own evaluations. At the time this issue was prepared, the GitHub and Hugging Face links on the blog both said "Coming soon". Weights, license, and code were not public. Whether it actually opens, and on what terms, has to wait for the release.
