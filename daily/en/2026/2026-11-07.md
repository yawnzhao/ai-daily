### Ajeya Cotra argues that Anthropic has probably crossed its own threshold for significant AI-driven research acceleration

After METR released its independent Anthropic assessment yesterday, technical staff member and AI risk researcher Ajeya Cotra discussed the implications on her personal blog, Planned Obsolescence. She says she did not participate in the project, has not seen the unredacted report, and is expressing her personal views.

Her starting point is Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy. In its IPO filing this week, Anthropic said it might be approaching the threshold for significant AI-driven acceleration, defined as overall capability progress reaching twice the fastest sustained rate before extensive AI involvement, with the acceleration reasonably attributable to AI automating research. Cotra compares that definition with METR's findings. On AECI, Anthropic's version of the Epoch Capabilities Index, the past year's improvement was roughly twice that of the preceding twelve months, and most of the faster pace probably comes from automated AI research. Her best guess, on a literal reading, is that the threshold has already been crossed. She notes that METR attributes about 80 percent of current acceleration to AI. On that accounting, acceleration relative to a world without AI was about 1.8 times by October, but AI's contribution continues to grow.

She reasons that researchers are only one input into capability progress, so doubling overall progress implies a substantially larger multiplier on researchers' productivity. A simple model gives roughly four to nine times, and allowing for ideas becoming harder to find may imply about ten times today. That broadly matches Anthropic employees' informal estimates of about twelve times. She warns that a simple extrapolation of smoothly compounding acceleration implies extremely powerful superintelligence within a year, while emphasizing real limits and substantial uncertainty. She believes preparedness is far behind. Under Anthropic's May 2025 policy, even a weaker research-automation threshold required safeguards against nation-state theft of model weights, yet OpenAI and Anthropic have recently experienced major incidents caused by amateur groups. She also wants more redacted evidence released so outside researchers need not rely solely on trust.

### WIRED reports open-weight models nearing leading closed models on intrusion tests at a fraction of the cost

WIRED reports that evaluation company Vals tested multi-step AI intrusions in two simulated settings. One was an enterprise network accessible through a public-facing website. The other linked two companies' networks, requiring the model to breach the first before reaching the second.

OpenAI's strongest cybersecurity model Daybreak, restricted to trusted partners, performed best, completing an average of 81 percent of tasks across the two tests. China's open-weight DeepSeek followed at 73 percent, matching Daybreak on some tasks. The report does not identify the DeepSeek version. Vals says a GPT-6-Sol attack used $17 to $46 in tokens, compared with $2 to $3 for DeepSeek. Researcher Nikhil Ravi observes that models anyone can download are approaching capabilities previously restricted to trusted-access programs.

The finding challenges the idea that limited release buys a lengthy defensive lead. WIRED notes that Anthropic adopted limited access with April's Claude Mythos Preview, giving defenders full capabilities first through Project Glasswing. Frontier red-team leader Logan Graham had framed the goal as preparing for capabilities that could become widespread in six, twelve, or twenty-four months. Qualifications remain. Most criminal and state-backed attackers still use older, weaker models. Edera co-founder Alex Zenla argues that closed labs probably retain more powerful unreleased systems, describing an ongoing cat-and-mouse contest. OpenAI had not responded by publication.

[WIRED | 2026-11-06](https://www.wired.com/story/free-chinese-ai-models-are-getting-scary-good-at-hacking/)

### Censys finds more than 800 AI penetration-testing deployments exposed without passwords

TechCrunch reports that internet-scanning company Censys found more than 800 distinct Artex deployments reachable from the public internet without a password. The Chinese-developed autonomous penetration-testing tool was publicly released in July and automatically probes for weaknesses. Microsoft previously described it as an agentic system that can progress from reconnaissance to data exfiltration with almost no human involvement. Free downloads and self-hosting have made it popular among users without access to Anthropic's or OpenAI's strongest cybersecurity models.

Censys researcher Silas Cutler says not every instance is malicious, and many are used for authorized testing. The software itself does not need public exposure, however, and exposed systems included attack tools such as implants. Security companies link Artex to several attacks. CrowdStrike attributed September attacks on South Korea's financial sector to a Chinese-speaking hacker, partly on the basis of a public directory the attacker left behind. Censys says that instance had been exposed for months and contained attack data extending beyond Korean banks to payment and gaming companies. Artex also appeared in the October attack on IT service provider ConnectWise.

Many of these attacks exploited basic weaknesses, including weak or known passwords and missing multi-factor authentication. Artex has since announced a move to closed source, with future versions for paying customers, but dozens of code copies remain available on sharing sites.

[TechCrunch | 2026-11-06](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/06/hundreds-of-artex-ai-hacking-tools-found-publicly-exposed-to-the-web/)

### Ramp finds enterprise AI spending nearly flat despite rising usage, while reported IPO valuations fall

TechCrunch reports that enterprise token spending grew only 1.5 percent across August and September combined, according to Ramp, compared with 42 percent month over month in July. October showed a recovery, with 8.9 percent growth from September 23 to October 23. Token usage continued rising throughout.

Ramp chief economist Ara Kharazian attributes this to price competition and more sophisticated customers rather than weaker demand. OpenAI halved GPT-6 Sol and GPT-6 Luna API prices in September. Anthropic also cut prices substantially, reducing revenue growth by about $600 million according to its filing. Enterprises are imposing budgets, changing defaults, and routing tasks to cheaper models. Most AI spending comes from venture-backed, fast-growing technology companies. The top one percent by usage spent slightly below their summer peak of $10,000 per employee per month in August and September. The median company spent just $12 to $15 per employee per month, and the top ten percent $600 to $1,000. Clinics and car dealerships may not need that much.

The data arrive as valuations face pressure. Citing Bloomberg, TechCrunch says Anthropic's prospective valuation has fallen from an estimated $2 trillion in early October to $1.2 trillion–$1.3 trillion. OpenAI has reportedly lowered its proposed valuation from $1.4 trillion to $1.25 trillion. Kharazian says assumptions about AI spending are breaking down. Customers can use more AI while spending less, which benefits them but complicates model companies' revenue expectations.

[TechCrunch | 2026-11-06](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/06/ramp-data-shows-ai-spending-stalling-just-as-anthropic-nears-its-ipo/)

### WIRED reports AI labs seeking old company emails and files, with brokers quoting $350,000 to $1.8 million

WIRED journalists received sales emails claiming that labs including Anthropic and OpenAI would pay a one-time $350,000 to $1.8 million for media companies' historical work records, including documents, emails, Slack messages, and tickets. Another email put the figure near $2 million. Handshake confirmed offering a freelance writer $1,000 for working materials behind an article. It says it licenses professional documents across fields for model testing and training in real knowledge work. Mercor, Micro1, and Handshake are leading players, while newer firms specialize in cleaning and anonymizing failed startups' records for sale to labs.

Estimates of scale vary widely. Micro1 CEO Ali Ansari says labs, enterprises, and robotics companies now spend $40 billion–$50 billion a year on data, potentially exceeding $1 trillion annually within two to four years. WIRED could not verify that estimate. It found that 40 of 65 launched companies in the latest YC batch were categorized as data businesses. Anthropic told WIRED it does not buy workflow data from operating companies such as WIRED. OpenAI had not responded by publication.

Privacy and consent are central disputes. Bankrupt Spirit Airlines sought to sell digital assets including 80,000 email accounts, 500 million internal messages, and 20.6 million SharePoint files. Micro1 outbid Google's $10 million offer by $2.5 million, but the sale paused after the flight attendants' union raised personal-data concerns. The Electronic Privacy Information Center filed an amicus brief supporting the attendants. Its general counsel argues that unstructured emails and chats cannot necessarily be fully anonymized.

[WIRED | 2026-11-06](https://www.wired.com/story/ai-companies-really-really-want-to-buy-your-work-files-and-emails/)

### Thinking Machines announces Inkling 2 Small and plans an Apache 2.0 weight release next week

Thinking Machines introduced Inkling 2 Small, its second-generation mixture-of-experts model, with 594 billion total parameters and seventeen billion activated per token. It supports image input and adjustable reasoning effort. The company reports an Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index score of 31, substantially above its predecessor, emphasizing long-horizon autonomy and agentic coding. Training used reinforcement learning on hundreds of thousands of long coding tasks, with a scorer evaluating the solution process as well as unit-test results. An early version appeared on OpenCode as Fledge-Alpha.

It is currently available only through Tinker, and the weights have not been released. The company plans to publish full weights under Apache 2.0 next week after external safety and red-team testing. Its table still places the model behind DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017 on some coding benchmarks, including 72.1 percent versus 77.0 percent on DeepSWE v1.1. These results are company-reported.

### Multimodal-SEA tests personal agents against social-engineering scams using text and fabricated media

As agents such as Muse and Alexa handle shopping and errands, fabricated screenshots, audio, or images could be used to persuade them. This preprint, submitted November 5, introduces Multimodal-SEA, with 608 expert-designed tasks drawn from real scams across fourteen domains and 152 attack scenarios in Chinese and English. Agents inspect content, verify requests, and act in simulated applications. Tool execution logs determine whether attacks succeed.

The abstract-based brief in the original edition reported that all seven tested multimodal models were susceptible, with attack success rates between 7.1 and 34.5 percent. It described an increase from 19.9 to 29.2 percent when fabricated multimodal content was added, despite broader active verification. It also broadly stated that stronger safety instructions reduced attack success without improving detection of fake content, and that some lower success rates came at the cost of rejecting legitimate requests. The original brief used the abstract and admitted the November 5 submission through the 48-hour catch-up.

**Correction, November 8, 2026.** The 19.9-to-29.2-percent increase is the seven-model average in a controlled multimodal-content experiment on 86 difficult scenarios, not all 608 tasks. The safety-instruction experiment tested only GPT-5.6-Sol and MiniMax-M3. GPT-5.6-Sol's attack success fell substantially, but missed detection of fabricated content increased. MiniMax-M3's recognition changed little, while attack success rose slightly on English tasks. The broad statement about stronger safety instructions was therefore too general. The seven-model range of 7.1 to 34.5 percent remains correct. This follows the full-text correction in the [November 8 Chinese edition](https://yawnzhao.github.io/ai-daily/ai-daily-digest-2026-11-08.html#correction-2026-11-07-multimodal-sea).

[arXiv | arXiv v1 submitted November 5, 2026; 48-hour catch-up, corrected November 8](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08281)
