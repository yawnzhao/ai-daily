Data centers

A judge denies xAI's motion to dismiss, and the NAACP suit over data-center turbine pollution goes on

TechCrunch reported on October 31 that a U.S. federal judge denied xAI's motion to dismiss a lawsuit by the NAACP. The Southern Environmental Law Center, suing for the NAACP, alleges that the company, now part of SpaceX, installed and ran at least 35 gas turbines and other air-pollution sources at the site powering the Colossus data center outside Memphis, without the required pre-construction and operating air permits, and in violation of the Clean Air Act.

xAI had argued that the turbines were mobile equipment and needed no permit, that the plaintiffs lacked standing, and that the citizen-suit provision is unconstitutional. Judge Debra M. Brown rejected each point. The plaintiffs have standing under the Clean Air Act. Under Mississippi's implementation plan the turbines are not a mobile source. A citizen suit is not an exercise of federal executive power. A similar motion to dismiss, filed earlier this month by the U.S. Department of Justice, was also denied. The NAACP's lead for environmental and climate justice described the ruling as another important win. TechCrunch has asked SpaceX for comment. The report notes that public feeling about data centers has turned sharply against them. New York State recently passed a pause on new data centers, and Amazon and Microsoft have promised to stop using nondisclosure agreements in talks with local governments.

Mathematics

Mathematician Calegari, on Terence Tao's blog: mathematics has changed, and hiring and evaluation should change with it

On November 1, Terence Tao published a guest post by number theorist Frank Calegari on his blog. The Chinese edition gives the title as mathematics having changed, and the field needing to act accordingly. The post URL carries the same sense. Calegari argues that OpenAI's release of more than 700 research papers on October 6 is a watershed in the history of mathematics, and that AI can already make breakthrough contributions in many areas. He also says the papers are badly written, do not follow the field's authorship norms, and that some are wrong. Rather than dwell on what a trillion-dollar company does, he thinks mathematicians should spend their effort where they have control: how they hire, how they publish, and how they train students.

He uses the classification of finite simple groups as an analogy. Once a famous problem is declared solved, the heat goes out of the field, but the problem itself is far from finished. He acknowledges that many people are now using AI to race to be the first to prove something, and that the ability to produce a proof is pulling away from the ability to understand the subject and move it forward. In his view, choosing the problem worth doing may matter more than producing the proof. He offers several concrete suggestions: evaluate every piece of work as if it might have been AI-assisted; hire more through face-to-face conversation, not only from the papers; require journals to adjust, and leave the ones that will not; give graduate training more breadth without dropping technical training; run better seminars; and take more responsibility for results that come from AI. In the Chinese edition's paraphrase, an optimist is told that the game has changed rather than ended.

Outdoors

WIRED: more hikers plan with a chatbot, and search-and-rescue teams see a new risk

WIRED on November 1 republished a High Country News report. Three 20-year-old hikers called for help on Mount Shasta in California. They later said that poor preparation and a flawed plan came partly from relying on Google's Gemini. They did not bring enough food, they overestimated their fitness, they did not turn back when they missed the summit in the afternoon, and they reached the top at 7:30 p.m. They had to descend in the dark, got lost the next morning, and one of them injured a knee. A U.S. Forest Service rescuer said the group really had done the homework, and also that they had somewhat switched their judgment off. The report also describes people who camped beside a no-camping sign because an AI said they could, and people carrying an AI-generated map that placed a landmark on the other side of the mountain. In British Columbia, a 16-year-old who planned a trip with Claude was lifted off a ridge by helicopter. He later said he had also used Google Maps and AllTrails.

The superintendent of Grand Teton National Park said that over the past year the share of visitors who said they got trip information from AI rose from zero to 26 percent. It is not yet clear that AI is raising the number of accidents. Wyoming had fewer search-and-rescue calls this year than last. Chris Flathmann, a Clemson professor who studies human-AI collaboration, warns that a chatbot will confidently offer a route that sounds plausible and may come from an outdated post or from something made up, and that once a person hands over judgment it is hard to think critically again. The experts' advice is to check the latest map from the public-land agency, a trail-association site, or a guidebook before going somewhere unfamiliar, and better still to ask a guide or a ranger.

Justin Solomon: after a machine can write a proof, mathematics cares more about taste and about the question

Justin Solomon is an associate professor of electrical engineering and computer science at MIT. He studies geometric data processing. This conversation is from Bloomberg's business podcast Odd Lots. The Concourse record dates the episode to October 9, 2026. What follows is a paraphrase of caption excerpts the Concourse page arranged from automatic subtitles, not a verbatim English quotation.

He thinks mathematics is, to a large extent, about taste, not only about writing proofs and checking conjectures other people proposed. What is interesting is the modeling: working out which question to ask, and what insight that question can bring. That is what AI tools cannot do yet. He also says AI-generated Lean proofs can run to hundreds of thousands of lines, too long for a person to check line by line, so the check falls to the verifier. Once a proof can be produced with no friction, the detours and insights along the way that might open a new field can get cut out. This is a view from three weeks ago, not a response to Calegari's post. Both of them put the weight on what question gets asked.

01A related paper: PUE is not enough, and a data center needs a community-facing local-impact audit
Data centersv1 submitted September 20, 2026; related-paper look-backarXiv abstract page; preprint, body not read, not peer reviewed

Why read it today: the xAI turbine lawsuit and OpenAI's wish to rename data centers point at the same question. What ruler should a community use for a data center? This preprint, submitted to arXiv on September 20, 2026, says that power usage effectiveness, PUE, along with water usage effectiveness and carbon usage effectiveness, each divides a facility's resource use by its IT energy use. It is not a new paper from today. Those ratios do not directly show whether local resources are tight, whether infrastructure has capacity, or what the social effects are. Data the authors cite say that in the first quarter of 2026 alone, about 130 billion dollars of U.S. projects were delayed or cancelled because of local opposition. The main worries were water, electricity demand, infrastructure capacity, and transparency, not internal efficiency.

The authors propose a local-impact audit in five groups: efficiency, water management, carbon emissions and renewable energy, compliance, and local disclosure. It is designed to be assessed each quarter and checked independently against public records. They demonstrate it on three facilities in Illinois that are in the middle of local policy disputes, and they discuss the data-access barriers to an independent check. The reading scope is the abstract. The paper has not been peer reviewed. It was selected from the Paper-Radar library.

Memdoor's open workflow team-solve: five Qwen3.8-27B roles solve problems together and match a paper's resultApache-2.0

Memdoor is a single-binary command-line agent for programming and automation, under the Apache-2.0 license. Its example workflow team-solve writes a paper's method as eight YAML files, with no code and no model specified: a planner, two idea generators, a solver, and a manager, plus a repair role. Each starts from a fresh context and talks only through files on disk. A script then runs the samples and decides success or failure. On the same model, Qwen3.8-27B, the author took the first 10 problems of the original paper's LiveCodeBench Hard list and ran each five times. The workflow averaged 9.8. The original authors' framework averaged 9.6. In the paper's own data, a single Fable 5 call scored 8.0. Fifty runs cost 35.79 dollars in total, with a median of about 40 minutes each.

The author also states the limits: only 10 problems were tested, not the paper's 100, and 9.8 against 9.6 is inside the error range, so the claim is only that the workflow matches the original framework. The problems are from spring 2025, and the model may have seen them in training. The Fable 5 column comes from the original authors' file and was not rerun in this framework. The example was already public in the repository in late October and drew discussion on Hacker News on October 31. This issue did not run the code.
