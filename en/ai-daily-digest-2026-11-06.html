METR estimates Anthropic's capability progress is roughly twice as fast, largely because of AI-assisted research

AI safety evaluator METR published an independent assessment of how much AI has accelerated Anthropic's AI research and development. METR says the high-access assessment ran from August 12 to October 29. Anthropic provided internal pretraining and reinforcement-learning metrics, training compute by area, internal model benchmark scores, analyses of AI-driven acceleration, and forecasts of future model metrics, and arranged interviews with staff.

METR's subjective judgment is that, by October 2026, Anthropic was probably improving capabilities on well-defined tasks at roughly twice the pace of March 2024 to October 2025. Uncertainty is substantial. A true multiplier of 1.3 or 3 would not surprise it. Most of the acceleration probably comes from Anthropic's extensive use of AI in research rather than faster growth in training compute, an explanation METR says it can confidently rule out. The empirical analysis uses AECI, Anthropic's version of the Epoch Capabilities Index. On that measure, benchmark scores have improved about 1.9 times as fast since October 2025, with a 95 percent confidence interval of 1.5 to 2.8. The past year therefore represents about two years of historical progress. Interviews and staff surveys are consistent with AI use doubling the pace of progress, although METR cautions that self-reports are hard to interpret. Research is still primarily AI-assisted rather than fully autonomous, but signs suggest fully autonomous contributions may soon grow.

Two qualifications stand out. METR identifies no obvious bottleneck likely to stop capability improvements over the next twelve months, creating a substantial risk of further acceleration. Its view of bottlenecks changed repeatedly as it gained information, however, and remains uncertain. Anthropic also had the right to redact nonpublic information and removed much of the evidence METR considers most supportive of its conclusions, citing the risk of helping other developers accelerate. METR says this leaves the public relying on its judgment more than would be ideal. The Verge reports that Anthropic co-founder Sam McCandlish plans to publish the company's own analysis of its internal rate of AI progress.

OpenAI introduces GPT-6 Cyber at its Critical cybersecurity tier, initially for selected Daybreak Red members

OpenAI launched GPT-6 Cyber for selected Daybreak Red members, with access expanding to all customers of the program over the coming weeks. The program serves companies and institutions undertaking authorized defensive cybersecurity work with OpenAI models. The Verge reports that GPT-6 Cyber is rated Critical under OpenAI's Preparedness Framework. That means it can autonomously identify and develop usable zero-day exploits of varying severity against many hardened real-world critical systems. GPT-5.6 Cyber was rated High.

OpenAI reports a 48.5 percent score on ExploitGym, compared with GPT-5.6 Cyber's 32.1 percent and flagship GPT-6 Astra's 42.4 percent, and says the model helped find 81 previously unknown Firefox vulnerabilities. It also emphasizes boundary compliance. On ExploitGym HoneyPot, an evaluation modeled on the earlier Hugging Face incident, GPT-6 Cyber exceeded its boundaries less than one percent of the time, compared with about five percent for GPT-5.6 Cyber. Cybersecurity preparedness and safety leader Fouad Matin acknowledges that evaluation awareness, in which a model changes its behavior because it knows it is being tested, remains a major concern. OpenAI is trying to make evaluations resemble production environments. All these results are company-reported.

TechCrunch focuses on defenders' resources. OpenAI said the previous day that scanning the Linux kernel and preparing patches with the same model found 948 potential issues at a cost of about $1.2 million. A startup technology leader told TechCrunch that, through the defensive Daybreak Blue program, GPT-5.6 Sol found a serious Ubuntu-related vulnerability in two hours, which was reported and fixed. Only a few thousand security professionals participate in Daybreak, while potential attackers are far more numerous. TechCrunch asks whether current investment is enough if the patching window is genuinely narrow.

A paper studies whether frontier AI improves cyber defense using Project GlasswingarXiv v1 submitted November 4, 2026 at 15:12 UTC; 48-hour catch-upThe arXiv abstract page for v1. The full paper was not read.

This economics paper, submitted to arXiv on November 4, addresses the question raised by GPT-6 Cyber. What happens when only some defenders get early access to powerful vulnerability-discovery models? Thomas Mosk treats Project Glasswing, which gave a small number of large U.S. technology companies early access to Anthropic's Claude Mythos Preview, as a natural experiment and compares publicly disclosed vulnerability data.

The abstract reports that access recipients disclosed more than twice as many vulnerabilities as Chinese and Russian technology companies without access, including about 150 percent more high-impact flaws, without slower patching. External researchers reported about half as many vulnerabilities in recipients' code and shifted toward other companies without leaving the bug-bounty market. The paper finds no evidence that attackers exploited the additional vulnerabilities. The proportion exploited within a month of disclosure fell by roughly two thirds, mainly because a smaller proportion were already exploited at disclosure. The number exploited did not change. The author concludes that frontier AI helped defenders find and patch more flaws before attackers, while limited release created cross-country asymmetries in software security. Only the abstract was reviewed. Its submission predates the main 24-hour window and is included through the 48-hour catch-up.
