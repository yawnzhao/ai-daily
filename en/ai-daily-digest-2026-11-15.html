South Korea confirms NVIDIA's supply plans after leadership change, while Taipei tracks its headquarters project

Ransomware groups use AI for phishing and negotiation. What do their attack claims actually measure?

Ransomware groups use AI for phishing and negotiation. What do their attack claims actually measure?

WIRED interviewed researchers who have tracked ransomware for years. Allan Liska of Recorded Future has seen attackers make greater use of language models to write phishing emails and assist in negotiations. People still direct the major groups' operations. Cynthia Kaiser, a former FBI official who now leads research at Halcyon, said international cases accounted for more of the recent increase. In her view, more natural writing in the target's language makes it easier to run scams across language barriers.

Researchers track the victims that groups name on their leak sites. The records can err in either direction. Some victims keep an attack private, and some groups exaggerate or claim the same attack twice. The nearly 3,000 entries that some researchers counted over the summer were claims. They offer a way to follow activity, but establishing a count of distinct attacks requires independent checks. The trend alone cannot show how much of the increase AI caused.

AWS counts failed attempts when it prices a coding agent's successful tasks

AWS counts failed attempts when it prices a coding agent's successful tasks

AWS published an example of how to test model routing on Amazon Bedrock. The NVIDIA NeMo Switchyard plugin uses an agent's recent tool calls and their results to choose between two candidate models. A separate routing method assigns each model a weight and chooses randomly, giving teams a comparison. The example uses LiteLLM as its gateway and requires the client to include a supported conversation history with each request.

The benchmark script runs tasks with the router and with individual models. Passing the tests counts as completing the task. For each group, the script divides the estimated cost of every attempt by the number of tasks that passed. A model with a cheap per-call rate can still cost more per success if it takes extra turns, retries repeatedly or fails outright. AWS describes the integration as experimental. Teams can use the method to measure their own workloads before choosing a model.

Max Jaderberg on drug discovery: what should carry over to the next experiment?

In a No Priors interview released November 12, Elad Gil asked Max Jaderberg, president of Isomorphic Labs, how researchers gradually work out a mechanism of action. Concourse summarizes the exchange at 00:07:14–00:08:00 in the original recording. Jaderberg described a recurring process. Researchers propose an explanation or a design, test it and revise it in light of the results. Experiments take time, and it can be difficult to identify which part of the feedback is worth acting on.

The interview suggests a next step for the ranking study in this issue. A benchmark can end when the measured rankings are revealed. A drug developer still has to decide which hypothesis to revise and where to spend the next experiment's budget. That connection is our editorial interpretation. The Concourse page identifies the source and the time range.

01Can AI rank the outcomes of wet-lab experiments before it sees the results?
Can AI rank the outcomes of wet-lab experiments before it sees the results?Preprint submitted November 11 and covered November 14Methods, Table 1, results and limitations in version 1 of the arXiv preprint. The experiments were not reproduced.

On November 14, QbitAI covered Science Prediction Universe, a benchmark introduced in a preprint submitted on November 11. Researchers give agents the materials, experimental conditions and measurement method, then ask them to rank candidates while the results are withheld. For example, which conditions will give the highest yield for the same reaction? An independent scorer compares the predicted order with measurements from the lab.

The benchmark spans chemistry, materials science, biology and medicine. It includes 100 public tasks from published papers and 16 private tasks from four sets of unpublished experiments. Each run has an eight-hour budget. Agents may retrieve information, install tools and run computations. Since agents might have encountered answers to public tasks in the literature, the researchers anonymized the tasks, screened tool outputs and reported private-task results separately.

In Table 1, Opus 5.5 had the highest task-adjusted mean score on public tasks, at 0.478. Only 44 tasks received scores for that configuration, and 23 exceeded the chance threshold for the task. Biosafety filters led several configurations to decline some tasks. The 0.478 measures ranking performance. Reading it as 47.8% accuracy would change the meaning of the metric. When the researchers compared two reasoning settings for GPT-6 Astra, runs at the higher setting took 4.8 times as long at the median, with no measured gain in ranking scores.

In some runs the researchers examined, an agent carefully calculated an accessible proxy that had little bearing on the experimental outcome it was asked to predict. There are limits to the comparisons. The private tasks cover only four sets of experiments, with wide intervals around the estimates. Each configuration combines a model, reasoning settings and tools, so differences cannot be attributed entirely to the model. The preprint has yet to undergo peer review.
